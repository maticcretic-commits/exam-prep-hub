    1. The 2026 TOEFL iBT: Format & Registration

    What changed on 21 January 2026, section by section, and how to book your seat

    The redesign you must prepare for

    On 21 January 2026 ETS rolled out the biggest redesign of the TOEFL iBT in its history. The test is now shorter (about two hours), partly adaptive, built around real-life campus communication as well as academic English, and scored on a new 1–6 band scale aligned with the CEFR. Any book, course or YouTube series made before 2026 describes a test that no longer exists — check the publication date of everything you study.

    The four sections (current format)

    SectionTask typesItemsTime

    ListeningListen and Choose a Response · Listen to a Conversation · Listen to an Announcement · Listen to an Academic Talkup to 47~29 min
    WritingBuild a Sentence · Write an Email · Write for an Academic Discussionup to 12~23 min
    SpeakingListen and Repeat · Take an Interviewup to 11~8 min

    Item counts and times are ETS base figures; the test adapts, so your exact count may vary slightly. Section order is fixed: Reading → Listening → Writing → Speaking. There are no scheduled breaks.

    How adaptivity works

    Only Reading and Listening are adaptive, in two stages. Module 1 (the routing module) is the same difficulty for everyone. Your performance there decides whether Module 2 is easier or harder. You can move between questions within a module, but once you advance to Module 2 you cannot go back. Practical consequence: the first module is the highest-leverage part of the whole test — careless early errors cap your ceiling, because an easier Module 2 cannot produce the top bands.

    Registering from India

    • Create an ETS account at ets.org/toefl and register online; choose the test-centre iBT or the Home Edition (same test, same fee, live human proctor at home).
    • The standard fee in India is about ₹18,000 including GST (it was ₹16,900 in 2024–25; fees change, so confirm the live figure in your ETS account before paying).
    • Book 2–3 months ahead of your application deadlines; popular centres in Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru fill early. Late registration (within a few days of the test) and rescheduling both cost extra.
    • Bring a valid passport as ID at Indian test centres; the name on your registration must match it exactly.
    • For the Home Edition you need a private room, a computer with camera and microphone, and stable internet. At home you write on a small whiteboard or a blank sheet inside a transparent sheet protector with an erasable marker — headsets are not permitted at home.

    Scores and reporting

    • Scores are available in your ETS account within about 72 hours.
    • Your fee includes four free score reports to institutions (select them by 10 p.m. on test day); extra reports cost about US$25 each.
    • Scores stay valid for two years. MyBest scores combine your highest section scores from all tests taken within the last two years.
    • During the January 2026 – January 2028 transition, your report shows both the new 1–6 bands and a comparable overall score on the old 0–120 scale, so universities still listing old cut-offs can read your report directly.

    2. Reading I: Complete the Words

    The new C-test task &mdash; 10 blanks per paragraph, exact spelling, no partial credit

    What it is

    Complete the Words is a classic C-test: you get a short academic paragraph (roughly 70–100 words). The first sentence is left completely intact; starting from the second sentence, the second half of every second word is deleted, leaving a stem of 3–5 letters plus a blank. There are exactly 10 incomplete words per paragraph, each scored 1 or 0 — spelling must be exactly right, including endings and doubled letters. There is no partial credit and no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a blank empty.

    The five-step method

    1. Read the whole paragraph first. The intact first sentence and the full context tell you the topic and the grammar of every gap.
    2. Fill the obvious ones. Function words and repeated content words are free points: if the text already contains photosynthesis, a blank photosynt_____ is not a mystery.
    3. Decide the part of speech. The words around the blank dictate whether you need a noun, verb, adjective or adverb — most errors are wrong word forms, not wrong ideas.
    4. Use collocations. Academic English runs on fixed partnerships: draw a conclusion, play a role, raise awareness, conduct research. The stem plus the partnership usually gives the word.
    5. Check endings. Plural -s, past -ed, -ing, -tion / -sion — the grammar of the sentence tells you the ending before the meaning tells you the word.

    Worked mini-example

    “Sleep plays a critical role in how the brain stores new information. During dee_ sleep, the bra__ replays pat_____ of activity th__ occurred while a per___ was awake.”

    • dee_ → deep (adjective before the noun sleep; only one letter fits the stem)
    • bra__ → brain (subject of replays; the topic word from sentence one)
    • pat_____ → patterns (replays patterns of activity — plural needed after the verb)
    • th__ → that (relative pronoun introducing the clause)
    • per___ → person (singular after a)

    Pacing and pitfalls

    • Budget 2–3 minutes per paragraph (about 10–15 seconds per blank). If a blank resists for 30 seconds, guess and move on — the clock is section-wide.
    • Do not trust the number of underscores to equal the number of missing letters; the blank width is not a reliable clue.
    • Watch American vs British spelling only if you are sure ETS uses the American form — it does: organize, behavior, center.
    • Expect 2–5 such paragraphs per test, mixed into the adaptive modules; they reward vocabulary depth more than any other Reading task.

    3. Reading II: Daily Life & Academic Passage

    Emails, notices and texts plus the classic academic passage &mdash; 2&ndash;3 questions per set

    Read in Daily Life

    These sets use real-world artefacts: emails, notices, bulletin-board postings, text-message chains, schedules, menus, invoices, adverts, forms and campus announcements. The text is short — 15 to 150 words — and each set carries 2 or 3 questions with 4 options. The visual layout is part of the item: read headings, bold lines, tables and timestamps, not just the sentences.

    Question types you will meet

    • Main purpose / intended audience — who is this written for and why?
    • Specific detail — the deadline, the price, the room, the condition. Scan, don't re-read.
    • What should the reader do — the required action.
    • NOT / EXCEPT — which option is not stated; eliminate the three that are.
    • Simple inference — one careful step beyond the text, never a leap.
    • Phrase in context — what a phrase means here, not in the dictionary.

    The trap-word scan

    Before answering, circle the small function words that flip meaning: not, only, may, must, except, unless, required, optional, prior to, no later than. Distractors are routinely built by swapping one of these. “Bookings are not required for the open reading area” does not mean bookings are impossible there — it means you can walk in.

    Read an Academic Passage

    The traditional TOEFL passage survives in shorter, punchier form. The tested skills are the classic ones:

    • Main idea / primary purpose — what the whole passage is doing (describing, explaining, comparing, arguing).
    • Detail — stated facts; match the question stem to the exact sentence.
    • Vocabulary in context — the meaning a word carries in this passage; academic usage often differs from everyday meaning.
    • Inference — what must be true given the text.
    • Rhetorical function — why the author mentions an example or a detail.

    Reading method for the adaptive section

    • Read the question stems before the passage so you know what to hunt for.
    • Skim for structure first (topic sentences, contrast markers like however, although, despite), then hunt details.
    • Never spend more than about 90 seconds on a single multiple-choice item; mark, move, and return if the module clock allows.
    • Because Module 1 routes you, accuracy on the first module outweighs speed — a rushed error early costs more than a slow correct answer.

    4. Listening: All Four Task Types

    Audio plays once. Notes are your only replay button.

    The section at a glance

    Listening gives you up to 47 items in about 29 minutes and is adaptive like Reading (Module 1 routes you to an easier or harder Module 2). Every recording plays once — no pause, no rewind. You may take notes throughout; at a test centre you get scratch paper and a pencil.

    The four task types

    • 1. Listen and Choose a Response — rapid-fire: hear a short spoken line, instantly pick the most natural reply. Tests real-time comprehension and conversational pragmatics (greetings, requests, suggestions, reactions). Decide fast; hesitation is the enemy.
    • 2. Listen to a Conversation — a campus dialogue (student–professor, student–staff). Questions target main topic, speaker purpose, key details and simple inferences such as attitude or what happens next.
    • 3. Listen to an Announcement — short institutional messages (deadlines, policy changes, event details). Listen for purpose, dates, requirements and consequences — the 5 Ws in compressed form.
    • 4. Listen to an Academic Talk — the classic mini-lecture. Track the central idea, supporting points, examples, and the speaker's stance; note contrast markers (however, although, despite, on the other hand) because questions love them.

    The note-taking system (T-method)

    Divide your paper into two columns. Left column: structure (topic → point 1 → point 2 → conclusion). Right column: details (names, numbers, dates, key terms). Use abbreviations and symbols:

    • w/ with, w/o without, b/c because, → leads to, ↑ increase, ↓ decrease, ? unclear, ! emphasis/contrast
    • Drop vowels in long words (govt, env, psych) and never try to transcribe — notes are triggers, not transcripts.
    • For conversations, note each speaker's problem and the proposed solution; for talks, note the thesis and one example per point.

    Question-attack drills

    • Main-idea questions: answer from your left column, not from a vivid detail you remember.
    • Detail questions: the answer is stated nearly verbatim; eliminate options that twist one word (always/never/only are classic twists).
    • Inference / attitude: one step beyond the words — tone of voice plus word choice. If the speaker says “That's one way to look at it,” they are politely disagreeing.
    • Function questions (why does the speaker say X?): the answer is the rhetorical job — to illustrate, to concede, to correct — not the content of X.
    • Predict the question while listening: when a speaker stresses a number, a name or a contrast, a question is coming.

    Pacing

    There is no per-item clock, but the section is. Rough guide: Choose-a-Response items in seconds, conversations/announcements about a minute of questions each, academic talks a little more. If you blank on an item, choose the option supported by your notes and move on — Module 1 accuracy decides your Module 2.

    5. Speaking: Repeat & Interview

    7 Listen-and-Repeat items, 4 Interview questions, 8 minutes &mdash; precision beats performance

    The section at a glance

    Speaking is the shortest section: up to 11 items in about 8 minutes, last on the test. There is no preparation time on any item — you hear, and you speak. Two task types, both scored on accuracy, pronunciation, fluency and appropriateness, with responses rated by automated scoring plus certified human raters.

    Task 1: Listen and Repeat (7 items)

    You hear one sentence and must repeat it exactly. This is a memory-and-pronunciation task, not a creativity task. The sentences grow longer and more complex as the task proceeds.

    • Chunk it. As you listen, group the sentence into 3–4 meaning chunks (subject | verb phrase | extra detail). Memory holds chunks, not words.
    • Shadow the intonation silently while listening — stress and rhythm carry half the score.
    • Never go silent. A fluent near-miss outscores a perfect sentence delivered after a long pause; start promptly even if the ending is uncertain.
    • Do not paraphrase. Changing significant to important loses the accuracy point that word carried.
    • Drill: pause any English podcast sentence, wait two seconds, repeat it word-for-word. Start with 8-word sentences and build to 15+.

    Task 2: Take an Interview (4 items)

    One topic, four questions, 45 seconds each. Typical arc: introduce yourself → describe an experience → give an opinion → speculate or advise. You cannot prepare content, but you can prepare structure:

    • Answer + reason + example. “I prefer studying in the library (answer), because the quiet helps me concentrate (reason). Last semester I prepared all my finals there and my grades improved (example).”
    • Fill the 45 seconds. Short answers cap your score — if you finish early, add a second example or a consequence.
    • Think in English. Translating from your first language creates pauses and word-order errors; simple direct sentences beat ambitious translated ones.
    • Sound like a person, not a script. Raters discount memorised, template-heavy answers; vary your connectors (actually, in fact, for instance, on the other hand).
    • Pronunciation priorities: word stress (PHOtograph vs phoTOgraphy), sentence stress on content words, and clear endings (-ed, -s). Accent is fine; unintelligibility is not.

    The scoring lens

    • Delivery: clear pronunciation, natural pace, few unnatural pauses.
    • Language use: correct grammar and appropriate vocabulary — errors are tolerated if meaning stays clear.
    • Topic development: full, coherent answers that address the question directly.
    • Because AI rates alongside humans, audible clarity (mic distance, steady volume) is a genuine scoring factor on test day.

    Two-week speaking drill plan

    Daily 20 minutes: 5 minutes of repeat-after-audio shadowing, 10 minutes answering one random interview-style question aloud with a timer (record yourself), 5 minutes listening back and fixing one recurring error. After two weeks most test-takers gain a full half band from fluency alone.

    6. Writing: Sentences, Emails & Discussion

    12 items in 23 minutes &mdash; grammar first, then structure, then ideas

    The section at a glance

    Writing packs up to 12 items into about 23 minutes and runs third, right after Listening. It moves from micro (grammar) to macro (composition): machine-scored sentence building, then two human-and-AI-rated productive tasks.

    Task 1: Build a Sentence

    You are given sentence fragments in jumbled order and must arrange them into a single grammatically correct sentence. It tests word order, articles, prepositions and clause structure — pure grammar under time pressure.

    • Find the subject first — the noun phrase that the sentence is about — then its verb.
    • Place time/place expressions at the start or end, never between subject and verb.
    • Check every junction: article + noun, preposition + noun phrase, subject–verb agreement.
    • Speed matters: these items are quick points — decide in under 30 seconds each and bank the time for the two writing tasks.

    Task 2: Write an Email (~7 minutes)

    A short scenario (e.g. a missed deadline, a request to a professor) plus three content points you must cover. Scored on task completion, organisation, tone and language.

    • Cover all three points — an elegant email missing one point loses more than a plain email covering all three.
    • Structure: subject line → greeting (Dear Professor Lee,) → purpose in sentence one → the three points in order → polite close (Thank you for your understanding. Best regards, …).
    • Tone: formal-polite. “I would be grateful if…” beats “I want…”; “I apologise for…” beats excuses.
    • Length: about 80–120 words is the sweet spot — completeness with control.

    Task 3: Write for an Academic Discussion (10 minutes, 100+ words)

    The signature writing task: a professor poses a question and two classmates post views. You contribute your own post — state a position, support it, and engage with the discussion.

    • Take a clear position in the first two sentences: “I agree with Ana that…” or “In my view, the opposite is true.”
    • Develop with reasons and one concrete example — a study, a campus observation, a personal experience.
    • Engage: reference a classmate by name (“As Daniel noted…”); this is explicitly what the task asks for.
    • Academic register: varied sentence structures, precise vocabulary, correct paragraphing (2–3 short paragraphs).
    • Minimum 100 words — under-length responses are penalised. Aim for 120–150.

    Universal writing rules

    • Plan 1–2 minutes, write, then reserve 1 minute to proofread — fixing two subject–verb errors is worth more than one extra sentence.
    • Type steadily; the interface is a basic text box — no spell-check, no grammar hints.
    • Memorise 10 flexible academic frames (“This is largely because…”, “A compelling example is…”, “While it is true that…, …”) and deploy them naturally.

    7. Academic Vocabulary Builder

    The 570 word families and the collocation habits that power Reading, Listening and Writing

    Why vocabulary is the master key

    Vocabulary is tested directly (Complete the Words, vocabulary-in-context questions) and indirectly everywhere else: you cannot follow an academic talk, write a discussion post, or repeat a complex sentence without it. The highest-yield list is the Academic Word List (Coxhead) — 570 word families that dominate university texts.

    Learn words in four dimensions

    For each new word, capture: meaning, word family (analyse → analysis → analytical), collocations (conduct/carry out research, not “do research” in formal writing), and one real sentence of your own. Four-dimensional words survive; dictionary definitions evaporate.

    The 60 highest-frequency academic stems

    analyse, approach, area, assess, assume, authority, available, benefit, concept, consist, constitute, context, contract, create, data, define, derive, distribute, economy, environment, establish, estimate, evidence, export, factor, finance, formula, function, identify, income, indicate, individual, interpret, involve, issue, labour, legal, legislate, major, method, occur, percent, period, policy, principle, proceed, process, require, research, respond, role, section, sector, significant, similar, source, specific, structure, theory, vary. If any of these is unfamiliar, it goes to the top of your list.

    Prefixes and roots: the decoding engine

    • Negation/reversal: un-, in-/im-/il-/ir-, dis-, de-, non-, anti-, counter-
    • Degree/size: over-, under-, sub-, super-/sur-, micro-, macro-, multi-, poly-
    • Position/order: pre-, post-, re-, inter-, trans-, ex-, co-/con-/com-
    • Key roots: dict (say), scrib/script (write), port (carry), mit/miss (send), duc (lead), spec (look), cred (believe), bene (good), mal (bad), chrono (time), geo (earth), bio (life)

    Collocations that mark academic English

    • draw / reach a conclusion · play a crucial role · raise awareness · pose a threat · meet a deadline · take into account · in contrast to · as a result of · with regard to · on the basis of
    • Verb + noun pairs: conduct research, gather evidence, present findings, address an issue, overcome an obstacle, achieve a goal, maintain a balance, exert influence.
    • Adjective + noun pairs: significant impact, considerable evidence, underlying cause, primary source, reliable data, widespread concern.

    The false-friend watchlist

    Academic English reuses ordinary words with technical meanings: discipline (a field of study), novel (new, original), substantial (large in amount), convention (accepted practice), register (level of formality), medium (means of communication), critical (analytical, or crucial), elaborate (detailed). In vocabulary-in-context questions, always choose the meaning that fits the sentence, not the most common dictionary meaning.

    A 15-minute daily routine

    5 min: 10 new words with collocations → 5 min: write 5 original sentences → 5 min: review yesterday's words (spaced repetition). 10 words × 30 days = 300 four-dimensional words — enough to move a full band in Reading.

    8. Grammar for TOEFL

    The sentence engine behind three sections &mdash; twelve rules that cover 90% of errors

    1. Subject–verb agreement

    The verb agrees with the grammatical subject, not the nearest noun: “The quality of the experiments was questioned.” Watch: each/everyone/neither (singular); a number of (plural) vs the number of (singular); quantities treated as one unit (“Ten dollars is enough”).

    2. Tense and sequence

    Simple past for finished time (in 2019, yesterday); present perfect for past-with-present-relevance (have recently discovered). In reported speech after a past verb, shift back — “She said she was tired” — unless the fact is timeless.

    3. Articles

    a/an by sound: an hour, a university. The for the specific, the previously mentioned, the unique, the superlative. No article for plural/uncountable nouns used generally: “Research shows…”

    4. Prepositions

    Fixed partnerships, learned as units: responsible for, comply with, differ from, interested in, capable of, depend on, familiar with, result in (cause) vs result from (caused by).

    5. Parallelism

    Joined items share form: “to analyse, compare and evaluate” — not “to analyse, comparing and evaluation”. Correlatives (not only…but also, either…or) must join identical structures.

    6. Conditionals

    Type 1: If it rains, we will cancel (real future). Type 2: If I had time, I would help (unreal present). Type 3: If she had studied, she would have passed (unreal past). Mixed: If he had trained, he would be fitter now.

    7. Passive voice

    be + past participle; the agent takes by. Academic writing loves it: “The samples were analysed…” Keep tense consistent: “was conducted”, not “was conduct”.

    8. Relative clauses

    who/whom/whose for people, which for things, that for either in defining clauses. No preposition stranding errors: “the topic on which we agreed” (formal) or “the topic we agreed on”. Comma = non-defining (“My brother, who lives in Pune, …”).

    9. Modifiers

    Put the modifier next to what it modifies. Dangling: “Walking to the lab, the results were lost” → “Walking to the lab, I lost the results.” Only moves meaning: “Only she passed” vs “She only passed”.

    10. Word forms

    Adjectives modify nouns (a significant result); adverbs modify verbs/adjectives (improved significantly). After linking verbs (seem, become, remain) use adjectives: “The task seems difficult.”

    11. Comparisons

    Compare like with like: “The climate of Pune is milder than that of Delhi.” Double comparatives (more better) and incomplete comparisons (“higher” than what?) are always wrong.

    12. Sentence boundaries

    No comma splices (“It rained, we stayed” → “It rained, so we stayed” or a semicolon), no fragments, and subject–verb–object order stays intact: adverbs of time/place go at the edges, never between subject and verb.

    9. Note-Taking System

    Your only replay button &mdash; abbreviations, layouts and what to write per task

    The golden rule

    Notes are triggers, not transcripts. If you are writing full sentences, you are missing the next idea. Aim to capture key nouns, numbers and relationships — roughly one short line per 15–20 seconds of audio.

    The abbreviation kit

    • Logic: → (leads to), ↑ (increase), ↓ (decrease), b/c (because), w/ (with), w/o (without), esp. (especially), e.g., i.e.
    • Judgement: + (advantage/pro), − (disadvantage/con), ? (unclear), ! (emphasis/contrast), ≈ (similar), ≠ (different)
    • People/places: first initial after first mention (Prof. R → R); drop vowels (govt, univ, env, psych, dev)
    • Numbers: write digits always; circle deadlines, fees and quantities — questions target them.

    Layout per listening task

    • Conversation: two columns — Speaker A problem | Speaker B solution. End with the agreed outcome.
    • Announcement: the 5 Ws stacked: WHO issued it, WHAT changes, WHEN (deadline!), WHERE, WHY.
    • Academic talk: an outline — thesis at top, then indented point → example chains. Mark contrast words with ! because inference questions live there.

    What NOT to write

    • Do not copy the question — it stays on screen.
    • Do not write during Listen and Choose a Response items; they are too fast and too short.
    • Do not decorate — arrows and abbreviations only; if a symbol slows you down, drop it.

    Notes in Reading and Writing

    In Read in Daily Life, underline (mentally) the action required, the deadline and any condition before looking at the questions. In Write for an Academic Discussion, spend your first minute noting position → 2 reasons → 1 example; in Write an Email, number the three content points in the margin and tick them off. In Speaking there is no note time — that is why the chunking method in the Speaking chapter replaces paper.

    The 10-day hand-speed drill

    Play a 3-minute academic talk daily. Take notes with the outline layout, then answer: thesis? two points? one example each? any contrast? Compare against the transcript. Within ten days your capture rate roughly doubles — and with it your Listening band.

    Pacing per section, the adaptive-module priority rule, and the logistics checklist

    The pacing table

    The adaptive-module priority rule

    Reading and Listening each open with a routing module of fixed difficulty that decides whether your second module is easier or harder. Within a module you can navigate freely, but you cannot return after advancing. Strategy:

    • Module 1 = accuracy over speed. Double-check flagged items here; a careless error early caps your ceiling for the whole section.
    • Module 2 = commit and move. Trust your routing; answer steadily and never leave items blank.
    • If Module 2 feels easier, do not panic — it may be the test calibrating, and strong performance still earns a strong band.

    Answer-everything policy

    There is no penalty for wrong answers on the multiple-choice and completion items — a blank is a guaranteed zero while a guess has a 25% chance. In the final 60 seconds of any module, fill every unanswered item with your best-supported guess.

    Test-day checklist (test centre)

    • Valid passport (India) with the exact name on your registration; arrive 30 minutes early.
    • Leave phones, watches and study material outside — the centre provides secure storage. No personal writing materials; scratch paper and pencil are issued.
    • Eat beforehand and use the restroom: there are no scheduled breaks in the two-hour test.
    • During check-in you will be photographed; your photo appears on the score report.

    Test-day checklist (Home Edition)

    • Private, quiet, well-lit room with the door closed; desk clear except computer, ID and whiteboard/transparent sheet + marker.
    • Test your camera, microphone, speakers and internet the day before; headsets are not allowed.
    • The human proctor will ask to see the room via camera — keep the space tidy and private for the full two hours.
    • Do not read questions aloud or cover your mouth; the proctor may flag it.

    The week before

    Take two full timed simulations (Reading → Listening → Writing → Speaking, no breaks) at the same hour as your real test. Sleep 7+ hours the last three nights — fatigue destroys Listening and Speaking first. The day before: light review only, no new material.

    11. Scoring 1&ndash;6 Explained

    The two-year transition (Jan 2026 – Jan 2028)

    Because universities spent decades publishing 0–120 cut-offs, ETS dual-reports during the transition: your score report shows the new 1–6 section bands and a comparable overall score on the old 0–120 scale. Always check whether the university wants an overall score, section minimums, or both.

    How each section is scored

    • Reading & Listening: machine-scored — every item counts equally within its section.
    • Speaking & Writing: scored by automated scoring plus certified human raters against published rubrics (delivery, language use, topic development for Speaking; task completion, organisation, language for Writing). This is why clarity, structure and completeness beat ornament.
    • You can request a score review (re-score) of the Speaking and Writing sections for a fee if a result looks off.

    MyBest scores

    MyBest takes your highest section score from all tests in the last two years and combines them into a superscore shown alongside your latest test. Retaking to fix one weak section is therefore a legitimate strategy — a 6 in Reading from March still counts in September.

    Target bands (typical university expectations)

    • 5.0–5.5 overall — competitive for most top-100 US/UK universities (old ~95–107).
    • 4.5 overall — meets the published minimum of many good universities (old ~86).
    • 4.0 overall — the floor for a large number of programmes (old ~72); below this, conditional/admissions pathways appear.
    • Section minimums (e.g. “no band below 4.5”, common for teaching assistantships) matter as much as the overall — a 5.5 average with a 3.5 in Speaking can still be rejected.
    • Always verify on the university's own admissions page; requirements change yearly and department pages override general graduate-school pages.

    Strategy 2026-02-12

    Group each sentence into 3–4 meaning chunks as you listen. Memory holds chunks, not words — and never paraphrase; exact repetition is the task.

    Strategy 2026-02-10

    Choose-a-Response: decide in seconds

    The rapid-fire listening items test real-time comprehension. Hesitation is the enemy — commit to the most natural reply and move on.

    Strategy 2026-02-08

    The T-method for notes

    Left column: structure (topic → points → conclusion). Right column: details (names, numbers, dates). This split answers both main-idea and detail questions.

    Strategy 2026-02-02
