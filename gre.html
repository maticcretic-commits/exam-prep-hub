    1. GRE overview, format & scoring

    The GRE General Test is the admissions exam for thousands of graduate and business programs worldwide. This chapter maps the full battlefield: what each section asks, how long you get, and exactly how the 130–170 scoring works — so every later chapter slots into place.

    What the GRE tests

    The GRE has three measures. Analytical Writing (one essay) tests whether you can build and support an argument in clear academic English. Verbal Reasoning tests reading comprehension, logical sentence analysis, and vocabulary in context — not memorised definitions in isolation. Quantitative Reasoning tests high-school-level arithmetic, algebra, geometry, and data analysis under time pressure, with an emphasis on reasoning over computation.

    The current format (shorter GRE, since September 2023)

    Total testing time is 1 hour 58 minutes, with 54 scored questions plus one essay:

    • Analytical Writing: 1 task — Analyze an Issue — 30 minutes, always first.
    • Verbal Reasoning: 2 sections, 27 questions total — Section 1: 12 questions in 18 minutes; Section 2: 15 questions in 23 minutes (41 minutes total).
    • Quantitative Reasoning: 2 sections, 27 questions total — Section 1: 12 questions in 21 minutes; Section 2: 15 questions in 26 minutes (47 minutes total).

    There is no unscored experimental section and no scheduled break in the current format. An on-screen calculator is provided for Quant.

    Scoring: 130–170 per section, 0–6 for writing

    Verbal and Quant are each reported on a 130–170 scale in 1-point increments. Your raw score (questions right) is converted through a section-level adaptive algorithm: your performance on the first section of Verbal/Quant decides whether your second section is easier or harder, and harder second sections unlock higher score ceilings. Analytical Writing is scored 0–6 in half-point increments by one human reader and one automated engine.

    Key facts: there is no penalty for wrong answers — never leave a question blank. Scores are valid for 5 years. ScoreSelect lets you send only your best set of scores to schools.

    Registration, fees, and score use

    Register at ets.org — the fee is about US$220 in most countries (it varies by location, so confirm at registration). The test runs year-round at test centres and as an at-home online test. Most programs treat the GRE as one factor among GPA, essays, and recommendations — check each school's published average scores to set your target (often 160+ per section for competitive programs).

    2. Text Completion strategies

    Text Completion (TC) asks you to fill 1–3 blanks in a short passage. It looks like a vocabulary test but behaves like a logic test: the sentence always contains enough clues to predict the blank before you look at the choices. Master the logic and vocabulary becomes the easy part.

    The anatomy of a TC question

    Each blank has three choices; a 2- or 3-blank question requires every blank right for credit — no partial marks. Blanks can be single words or phrases. The sentence around the blank is engineered with clue words: support signals (indeed, moreover, because, similarly) telling you the blank continues the idea, and contrast signals (however, although, despite, surprisingly) telling you it reverses it.

    The MATH strategy (predict before you peek)

    1. Read the whole sentence and identify the idea without the blank.
    2. Decide the relationship: does the blank support or contrast that idea? Is the idea positive or negative?
    3. Predict your own word — even a simple one (good/bad, more/less) — before looking at the choices.
    4. Match: pick the choice closest to your prediction; eliminate anything that clashes in connotation.

    Covering the choices while you predict is the single highest-value habit in TC. Students who read choices first get seduced by familiar-sounding traps.

    Two- and three-blank questions

    Attack blanks one at a time, starting with the blank whose clues are clearest — often the second blank constrains the first. For each blank, recycle the other blanks as context, not as blanks to solve simultaneously. In 3-blank questions the three blanks usually form a logical chain (e.g., cause → description → consequence); find the anchor blank with the strongest clue words first.

    Classic traps to recognise

    • Near-synonym pairs where only one matches the sentence's degree (annoyed vs incensed).
    • Second-definition words: check (restrain), flag (weaken), qualify (limit), table (set aside).
    • Recycled words from the sentence that sound right but reverse the logic.
    • Extreme language (always, never, completely) when the sentence is moderate.

    3. Sentence Equivalence strategies

    Sentence Equivalence (SE) gives you one sentence with one blank and six choices — you must pick the <strong>two</strong> that produce sentences with the same meaning. Both must be right for credit. It rewards pairing logic more than raw vocabulary size.

    What SE really asks

    The two correct answers need not be strict synonyms — they must make the completed sentence mean the same thing in context. Often the test uses near-synonyms (hasty / impetuous), but sometimes two different words converge in context (frugal / economical). If no two choices pair up, you have misread the sentence.

    The pairing strategy

    1. Predict the blank using support/contrast logic, exactly as in Text Completion.
    2. Pair the choices first: group the six options into synonym pairs before matching to your prediction. The correct pair is usually visible once choices are grouped.
    3. Test each candidate pair by reading both completed sentences — they must say the same thing.

    Warning: two words can be synonyms of each other yet both be wrong if neither fits the sentence's logic (joyful / elated in a sentence demanding a negative word).

    Common SE traps

    • Synonym decoys: a pair that matches each other but not the blank's meaning.
    • One right, one wrong: the single best-fitting word paired with a near-miss — you need both.
    • Connotation flips: notorious vs famous — same denotation, opposite feeling.
    • Partial matches: a word that fits grammatically but contradicts a contrast clue.

    Building SE-ready vocabulary

    SE rewards clusters of related words, not isolated flashcards. Learn words in synonym families (e.g., laconic, taciturn, reticent = quiet; profligate, prodigal, spendthrift = wasteful) and always note connotation (positive/negative/neutral). Chapters 5 and 6 build these clusters systematically.

    4. Reading Comprehension

    Reading Comprehension (RC) is about half of every Verbal section: short passages (1 paragraph), medium passages, and long passages (several paragraphs), each followed by 1–4 questions. GRE passages are dense and boring by design — your job is structural reading, not memorisation.

    Passage types and question types

    Passages cover natural science, social science, arts, and humanities. Question types: main idea / primary purpose (what the author is doing), detail (what the passage states), inference (what must be true), function (why a sentence or example appears), attitude/tone, and select-in-passage (click the sentence that matches). About 13 of the 27 verbal questions are RC.

    Structural reading: the 3-pass method

    1. First pass (skim for structure): read for the author's claim and structure — thesis, evidence, concession, conclusion. Note where things are, not what they say in detail.
    2. Map as you go: one short note per paragraph (e.g., 'P1: old theory; P2: new evidence; P3: author qualifies').
    3. Attack questions by type: return to the passage for detail/inference questions; answer main-idea questions from your map, not from memory of details.

    Answering each question type

    • Main idea: must cover the whole passage — eliminate choices that are too narrow (one paragraph) or too broad.
    • Inference: must be directly supported; if you need an extra assumption, it's wrong. 'Most strongly supported' still needs textual backing.
    • Function: ask 'why did the author put this here?' — to concede, to exemplify, to contrast.
    • Detail: find the exact lines; the right answer paraphrases them. Beware choices that quote passage words but twist the meaning.

    The classic wrong-answer patterns

    • True but not asked: factually correct per the passage, but answers a different question.
    • Half-right, half-wrong: starts accurate, then adds an unsupported twist.
    • Extreme language: always, never, completely — the GRE loves qualified claims.
    • Out of scope: plausible in real life, but not in the passage.

    5. High-frequency GRE vocabulary I

    GRE vocabulary rewards words that appear across academic writing — precise, slightly formal, and often with a second meaning. This chapter covers the first 60 high-frequency words in meaning clusters, each with a definition and a memory hook. Learn each cluster as a unit.

    Words about honesty and deception

    • candid — frank, outspoken (a candid assessment). Hook: 'can did' — can say it, did say it.
    • disingenuous — not sincere, pretending ignorance. Hook: dis + ingenuous (naive).
    • duplicity — deceitfulness, double-dealing.
    • equivocal — open to more than one interpretation; deliberately vague. Contrast with unequivocal (clear).
    • veracity — truthfulness, accuracy. Veracious = truthful.

    Words about praise and criticism

    • laud — praise highly. Laudatory = expressing praise.
    • extol — praise enthusiastically.
    • disparage — regard as being of little worth; belittle.
    • deride — mock with contempt. Derisive = mocking.
    • castigate — reprimand severely.
    • opprobrium — public disgrace from shameful conduct.

    Words about knowledge and ignorance

    • erudite — having great knowledge. Erudition = scholarship.
    • pedantic — excessively concerned with minor details or rules.
    • esoteric — intended for a small, specialised audience; obscure.
    • abstruse — difficult to understand; obscure.
    • perspicacious — having keen insight and judgement.
    • sagacious — wise, showing good judgement.

    Words about change and stability

    • mercurial — subject to sudden mood changes; volatile.
    • capricious — given to sudden changes of mood or behaviour.
    • immutable — unchanging over time.
    • transient — lasting only a short time; impermanent.
    • ephemeral — lasting a very short time (even shorter than transient).

    Words about carefulness and carelessness

    • meticulous — showing great attention to detail; very careful.
    • assiduous — showing great care and perseverance.
    • perfunctory — carried out with minimum effort; half-hearted.
    • cursory — hasty and not thorough.
    • slipshod — careless, sloppy work.

    Words about stubbornness and flexibility

    • intransigent — refusing to change one's views; uncompromising.
    • obdurate — stubbornly refusing to change.
    • recalcitrant — resisting authority or control.
    • tractable — easy to control or influence; docile.
    • malleable — easily shaped or influenced.

    Words about generosity and greed

    • magnanimous — generous and forgiving, especially to a rival.
    • altruistic — selflessly concerned for others.
    • munificent — extremely generous (larger than life).
    • parsimonious — unwilling to spend; stingy.
    • avaricious — extremely greedy for wealth.

    Words about beginnings and originality

    • nascent — just coming into existence; emerging.
    • incipient — in an initial stage; beginning to happen.
    • novel — new and original (second meaning beyond the book).
    • hackneyed — overused, therefore unoriginal; clichéd.
    • derivative — imitative of others' work; unoriginal.

    Words with tricky second meanings

    • check — to restrain or hold back (checked his anger).
    • flag — to weaken or diminish (his energy flagged).
    • qualify — to limit or modify a statement (she qualified her praise).
    • champion — to defend or support a cause.
    • plastic — easily shaped or influenced (adjective).
    • pedestrian — dull, ordinary, unimaginative.
    • wanting — lacking (the report was wanting in detail).
    • tell — to reveal (her smile told of relief).

    How to actually retain them

    Three rules that work: 1) learn in clusters (above), not alphabetical lists — the GRE tests words in contrasting contexts. 2) always learn the connotation (+/−/neutral) alongside the definition; half of TC/SE questions are solvable on connotation alone. 3) use each word in your own sentence within 24 hours — production beats recognition. Review on a spaced schedule (1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days); GregMAT's Vocab Mountain (in Resources) is built exactly for this.

    11. Analytical Writing (AWA)

    One 30-minute essay: Analyze an Issue. You are scored 0–6 on how well you construct and support an argument — not on which side you take. A strong template plus practiced structure reliably earns 4.5+.

    What the task asks

    You receive a statement of general interest ('Governments should fund scientific research even when it has no immediate practical application') and must respond with your own position, supporting it with reasons and examples. There is no 'correct' side — both positions can score 6. The old Argument task was removed; only the Issue essay remains.

    The 0–6 scoring rubric

    • 6: cogent, well-articulated analysis; compelling reasons; fluent, precise language; few if any errors.
    • 5: generally thoughtful analysis; well-developed reasons; facility with language; minor errors.
    • 4: competent analysis; adequate reasons; adequate control of language.
    • 3 and below: limited analysis, weak organisation, poorly supported claims, frequent errors.

    Scoring is holistic: organisation + development + language. Length correlates with score only because developed arguments need words — padding without ideas earns nothing.

    A reliable 30-minute structure

    1. Minutes 0–3: plan. Pick your side, list 2–3 reasons, pick one concrete example per reason (history, current events, personal observation all count).
    2. Paragraph 1 — introduction: restate the issue, state your position clearly, preview your reasons.
    3. Paragraphs 2–3 — body: one reason each; claim → explanation → concrete example → tie-back to thesis.
    4. Paragraph 4 — concession: acknowledge the strongest opposing point, then explain why your position still holds — this is what separates 4s from 5s and 6s.
    5. Paragraph 5 — conclusion: restate position and reasons in fresh words.
    6. Minutes 27–30: proofread for subject-verb agreement, fragments, and typos.

    Language that scores

    • Use transitions that show logic: however, consequently, for instance, admittedly, nevertheless.
    • Vary sentence length; avoid repeating the same sentence starter.
    • Prefer precise verbs (undermines, reinforces, illustrates) over vague ones (does, gets, shows).
    • ETS publishes scored sample essays with commentary — read the 6s and the 3s to calibrate (link in Resources).

    12. Test-day strategy & pacing

    The GRE rewards strategy as much as knowledge: section-level adaptivity, no guessing penalty, and tight timing make pacing decisions worth real points. This chapter turns everything into an executable game plan.

    Pacing targets

    • Verbal: ~1.5 minutes per question. Section 1: 18 min for 12 Q; Section 2: 23 min for 15 Q. RC passages: budget ~8 minutes per long passage set, ~2 minutes per single-question short passage.
    • Quant: ~1.75 minutes per question. Section 1: 21 min for 12 Q; Section 2: 26 min for 15 Q. If a question passes 2.5 minutes, guess and move — one question is never worth two.
    • Checkpoints: at the halfway time mark you should be halfway through the questions. Wear a simple watch habit: glance at the clock every 5 questions.

    How adaptivity shapes your strategy

    Your first Verbal and Quant sections determine your second section's difficulty — and only the hard second section unlocks the top score band. So the first section of each measure is the highest-leverage 20 minutes of your test: slow down slightly, double-check, and protect accuracy there. On the second section, difficulty tells you how you're doing — a brutal section 2 means section 1 went well.

    Question tactics

    • Never leave a blank: no guessing penalty — eliminate, then guess.
    • Quantitative Comparison: compare, don't compute. Test positive/negative/fraction/zero/extreme cases; the answer is D ('cannot be determined') more often than test-takers expect.
    • Multiple-answer questions: evaluate each choice independently — 'select all that apply' has no partial credit.
    • Numeric entry: no choices to backsolve from; estimate to sanity-check your answer.
    • Mark and return: flag time-sinks and come back if time remains — easy questions later in the section are worth the same points.

    The week before and test day

    • Last 2 weeks: taper — two full timed POWERPREP tests, error-log review, light vocab maintenance. No new material in the final 48 hours.
    • Day before: confirm ID requirements and centre/at-home setup; sleep beats cramming.
    • Test day: arrive 30 minutes early; bring valid ID; there is no scheduled break, so pace yourself to go straight through.
    • At-home testers: test your equipment days ahead, clear your room per ETS rules, and keep your ID within reach.

    Score goals and retakes

    Set targets from real program data (published class profiles), not folklore. A balanced 160/160 beats a lopsided 165/155 for most programs. If you retake, ScoreSelect lets schools see only your best sitting — and ETS data shows focused 4–8 week prep between sittings is where retake gains come from, not from rushing back in.

    In TC, a colon or dash often defines the blank outright ('...: every word earns its place'). The test literally hands you the answer — take it.

    Vocabulary 2026-02-26

    The 'ob-' family

    Obdurate (hardened against), obfuscate (darken against = confuse), obstreperous (roaring against = unruly). Against-words for resistance.

    Strategy 2026-02-26

    One sitting per week

    A full timed sitting weekly builds the specific endurance the GRE demands. Midweek: targeted drills. Weekend: the full simulation.

    Format 2026-02-24

    Test centres year-round

    Slots are frequent but popular dates fill. Book when your prep has a shape — 6 to 8 weeks of runway is the sweet spot.

    AWA 2026-02-24

    Don't memorize essays

    Memorized sentences that don't fit the prompt hurt more than they help. Memorize structure and transitions; generate content fresh.

    Verbal 2026-02-22

    Word roots > word lists

    An hour on 40 roots and affixes unlocks thousands of words; an hour on 40 isolated words unlocks 40. Do the math on your study hours.

    Quant 2026-02-22

    Function notation

    f(g(2)): inside out. Composite functions are just ordered substitution — slow down and do one layer at a time.

    Vocabulary 2026-02-20

    The 'super-/sur-' family

    Supercilious (above-browed = contemptuous), surfeit (over-fullness), surreptitious (secret). Above-words, often looking down.

    AWA 2026-02-16

    Topic sentences carry paragraphs

    Each body paragraph's first sentence states its reason plainly. A reader should grasp your whole argument from the topic sentences alone.

    Verbal 2026-02-16

    Read the whole sentence

    TC blanks are decided by the whole sentence, not the five words around the blank. The clue is often at the far end — 'despite' in the subordinate clause.

    Strategy 2026-02-14

    Track time per question type

    Log average time on TC vs RC vs QC vs numeric entry. Your slowest type is where pacing drills pay most.

    Vocabulary 2026-02-12

    The 'inter-' family

    Internecine (mutually destructive), interpolate, interlude. Between-words — useful for the GRE's favorite relationship.

    Quant 2026-02-12

    Averages: find the total

    Stuck on an average problem? Multiply out to the total — most average puzzles are total puzzles wearing a disguise.

    Format 2026-02-10

    Score reporting

    You see unofficial Verbal/Quant scores immediately; official scores (with AWA) follow in about two weeks. Plan application timelines around that.

    Verbal 2026-02-10

    Eliminate with connotation

    Can't define all five choices? Eliminate by +/− feeling. Three eliminations make a guess; two eliminations make a good guess.

    Quant 2026-01-30

    Slope facts

    Parallel: equal slopes. Perpendicular: product −1. Horizontal: 0. Vertical: undefined. Four facts, constant use.

    Verbal 2026-01-30

    The 'indeed' amplifier

    'Indeed', 'in fact', 'truly' intensify the sentence's direction — the blank doubles down, not sideways. Amplifiers demand strong matches.

    Vocabulary 2026-01-28

    The 'de-' family

    Deplete, denigrate, deride, deplore — 'de-' (down, away) drags words downward. Down-words for decline and criticism.

    Strategy 2026-01-28

    Start with the format

    Before any content study, learn the test's shape: sections, timing, adaptivity, scoring. Strategy built on the format beats content without a plan.

    Verbal 2026-01-24

    The dictionary is not the test

    Knowing a word's first definition is table stakes. The GRE tests second meanings, connotations, and shades — study words the way the test uses them.

    Quant 2026-01-24

    Percent problems: identify the whole

    Every percent question hides a 'whole'. Find it first ('percent of WHAT?') and the equation writes itself.

    Strategy 2026-01-22

    Consistency beats intensity

    Ninety focused minutes daily for eight weeks beats heroic weekend marathons. The GRE rewards the compounding of small daily gains.

    Vocabulary 2026-01-22

    The 'con-/com-' family

    Confluence, coalesce, complacent, concur — together-words. Note complacent's trap: 'together-pleased' became smugly self-satisfied.

    Quant 2026-01-20

    Check extremes in QC

    When variables are unconstrained, test the extremes: huge, tiny, negative, fractional. The answer often flips at the boundary.

    Format 2026-01-18

    One test, many doors

    Thousands of graduate and business programs accept the GRE worldwide — including many MBA programs. One preparation, many applications.

    Verbal 2026-01-16

    Build your signal-word reflex

    While reading anything — news, essays, papers — circle contrast and support words. The reflex transfers directly to TC/SE speed.

    AWA 2026-01-16

    The 30-minute budget

    3 minutes planning, 22 writing, 3 proofreading, 2 buffer. Rehearse the budget until it's automatic — time panic ruins good structure.

    Strategy 2026-01-12

    Your first full test is diagnostic

    Take one timed POWERPREP before serious prep. The score tells you where the points are — study what the test says, not what you fear.

    Vocabulary 2026-01-10

    The '-ous' ending

    Copious, supercilious, fractious, egregious — '-ous' means 'full of'. When you see it, ask: full of WHAT? The stem answers.

    Quant 2026-01-08

    Memorize squares to 25²

    1, 4, 9, ... 625. Instant square recognition speeds factoring, roots, and QC estimation more than any other single drill.

    Verbal 2026-01-06

    Read boring things well

    GRE passages are dense by design. Practice structural reading on dry material — journals, reports — so test-day prose feels familiar.

    Vocabulary 2026-10-01

    Oct 1 word drill: 7 academic verbs that answer themselves

    If you know the ROOT you know the word. mitigate (mitis = soft) = soften; obfuscate (ob + fuscus = dark) = make unclear; corroborate (cor + robur = strength) = support; refute (re + futare = beat back) = disprove; eschew (German scheuen = avoid) = shun; propound (pro + ponere = put forth) = propose; gainsay (against + say) = deny. Drill: use each in one GRE-style sentence today.

    Quant 2026-10-01

    QC trick: plug in three kinds of numbers

    Stuck on a Quantitative Comparison? Plug in a positive, a negative, a fraction between 0 and 1, and a large number. If the relationship between Column A and B changes with different legal values, the answer is D — cannot be determined. Example: Column A = x², Column B = 2x. Try x = 1, x = 3, x = -1, x = 1/2 — the winner keeps changing, so answer D.

    Verbal 2026-10-01

    Text Completion: find the turn word first

    Before predicting any blank, locate the structural clue: 'although', 'however', 'despite', 'yet' signal a CONTRAST; 'because', 'since', 'indeed', 'thus' signal SUPPORT. The turn word tells you whether the blank echoes or opposes the clue. Drill today: 10 double-blank TC items, predicting the blank in your own word BEFORE looking at choices.

    Verbal 2026-10-01

    Sentence Equivalence: near-synonyms in CONTEXT

    SE pairs must produce the same meaning for the sentence — 'cogent' and 'compelling' work together only if both fit the blank's logic. Trap: two answer choices that are synonyms of each other but BOTH wrong for the sentence. Always test: does each choice, plugged in alone, make the sentence say the same thing?

    Strategy 2026-10-01

    The 30-minute Issue essay budget

    AWA Issue task = 30 minutes. Budget: 4 minutes planning (position + 3 reasons + examples), 20 minutes writing (5 paragraphs), 6 minutes proofreading. Graders reward a clear position, logical structure and varied sentence length — not ornate vocabulary. Template: thesis in paragraph 1, one reason per body paragraph, concession + rebuttal in paragraph 4.
