    1. GMAT Focus: Overview, Format & Scoring

    The whole exam in one chapter: 2h 15m, three 45-minute sections, 205–805 scoring.

    The GMAT Focus Edition (launched 1 February 2024; the only GMAT since) is a 2-hour-15-minute computer-adaptive test with three sections, each 45 minutes: Quantitative Reasoning (21 questions), Verbal Reasoning (23 questions) and Data Insights (20 questions) — 64 questions total. You may take the sections in any order you choose.

    • Total score: 205–805, in 10-point steps — Focus scores always end in 5 (645, 705, 805). A score ending in 0 is an old-format score.
    • Section scores: 60–90 each, in 1-point steps. All three sections count equally toward the total (the old GMAT ignored IR and AWA).
    • Removed vs. the old GMAT: the essay (AWA) is gone, Sentence Correction is gone, and most geometry is gone. Data Sufficiency moved into Data Insights.
    • Fee: about $275 at a test centre (online delivery costs more in some regions); scores are valid for 5 years; you may test up to 5 times in 12 months (8 lifetime), with 16 days between attempts.
    • Question review: you can bookmark questions, review and change up to 3 answers per section — a safety valve the old GMAT never had.
    • Calculator: on-screen calculator only in Data Insights, never in Quant.

    Adaptive engine. The test is question-adaptive within each section: your performance steers the difficulty of the next questions. Early questions set your range, so accuracy on the first third of a section matters disproportionately — but with answer-changing allowed, never leave a question blank out of panic.

    Why DI now counts. Business schools asked GMAC for a test of data literacy; Data Insights now contributes a full third of your score. Quant-only preparation no longer works — a lopsided profile (e.g. Q88/DI66) is heavily penalised relative to balanced section scores.

    2. Quant: Arithmetic & Number Properties

    3. Quant: Algebra & Word Problems

    Linear/quadratic equations, inequalities, functions, sequences, and the word-problem translation engine.

    Algebra on the Focus edition is tested mostly through word problems: translating English into equations is the real skill. Roughly half of Quant questions are word problems in disguise.

    Core algebra moves

    • Linear systems: solve by substitution or elimination. Two equations, two unknowns — check the question asks for both; often it asks for a combination (e.g. x + y), which may be found without solving fully.

    4. Verbal: Critical Reasoning

    Arguments, assumptions, strengthen/weaken, inference, boldface — the logic engine of the Verbal section.

    Critical Reasoning is about two-thirds of the 23 Verbal questions. Every CR argument has the same anatomy: premises (stated facts), a conclusion (the claim), and an unstated assumption (what must be true for the conclusion to follow). Your job is almost always about the assumption.

    The question types and what they want

    • Strengthen: find the missing link that makes the conclusion more likely — new information that supports the assumption.
    • Weaken: attack the assumption: show the premises don't guarantee the conclusion (alternate cause, broken analogy, unrepresentative sample).
    • Assumption (necessary): use the negation test — negate each choice; the assumption is the one whose negation destroys the argument.
    • Inference / "must be true": the answer is directly provable from the stimulus — never bring outside knowledge. "Must be true" answers are boring and safe.
    • Resolve the paradox: both facts are true — find the missing piece that lets them coexist. Don't dispute the premises.
    • Evaluate: the answer is the question whose answer would most help assess the argument (often a "whether" question about the assumption).
    • Boldface: describe each bolded part's role (premise, conclusion, counter-premise, intermediate conclusion). Answer choices are abstract — paraphrase them into plain English.
    • Flaw: name the logical error: confusing correlation with causation, part-to-whole, equivocation, ad hominem, circular reasoning.

    Master process

    1. Read the question stem first — know what you're hunting.
    2. Identify the conclusion (indicator words: therefore, thus, hence, consequently, so).
    3. Paraphrase the argument in your own words; state the assumption before looking at choices.
    4. Eliminate: out-of-scope, opposite-effect, and "true but irrelevant" choices first.

    Classic traps: extreme language (always/never/only) in strengthen/weaken choices; answers that restate a premise; answers that strengthen the opposite conclusion; and choices that require an extra unstated step beyond the assumption.

    5. Verbal: Reading Comprehension

    Structural reading: purpose, point of view, inference, and detail — without re-reading the passage.

    RC is roughly one-third of Verbal: 3–4 passages (200–400 words each) with 3–4 questions each, in 45 minutes shared with CR. Passages cover science, social science, history and business. You cannot highlight much and there is no going back to earlier sections — read once, structurally.

    Read for structure, not facts

    • Track the author's purpose per paragraph: describe a phenomenon? present a theory? critique it? propose a solution?
    • Track point of view: whose opinion is this — the author's, a researcher's, a critic's? GMAT loves "the author would most likely agree" questions that hinge on POV.
    • Note contrast words (however, although, despite, yet) — the main point almost always sits right after a contrast.
    • Skip examples on first read; know where they are, not what they say. Detail questions send you back with line references.

    Question types

    • Primary purpose: what the author did, not the topic — "to evaluate two competing explanations", not "about dinosaurs". Eliminate choices that are too narrow (one paragraph) or too broad.
    • Detail / "according to the passage": find the exact lines; the answer is nearly verbatim. Beware one-word twists (most/some, always/usually).
    • Inference: must be strictly provable from the text — a mathematical deduction, not a plausible guess. Correct inference answers are conservative.
    • Function ("why did the author mention X"): answer in terms of the paragraph's job, not X's content.
    • Strengthen/weaken the argument in the passage: same logic as CR, applied to the author's reasoning.
    • Vocabulary in context: substitute each choice into the sentence; the passage's meaning decides.

    Timing: 3–4 minutes to read, ~1:30 per question. If a detail question resists after one targeted re-read, eliminate aggressively and move on — RC questions are worth the same as CR questions.

    6. Data Insights: Data Sufficiency

    The AD/BCE decision tree, value vs. yes/no questions, and the traps that define the DI section.

    Data Sufficiency moved from Quant into Data Insights on the Focus edition. Each DS question gives a question stem plus two statements; you judge whether the statements — alone or together — answer the stem. The five answer choices are always the same:

    • (A) Statement 1 alone sufficient, Statement 2 alone not.
    • (B) Statement 2 alone sufficient, Statement 1 alone not.
    • (C) Both together sufficient, neither alone.
    • (D) Each statement alone sufficient.
    • (E) Statements together still not sufficient.

    The AD/BCE tree

    Evaluate Statement 1 first: if sufficient, the answer is A or D (AD). If not, the answer is B, C or E (BCE). Then evaluate Statement 2 independently — never carry information from Statement 1 into Statement 2 (the classic cross-contamination error). Combine only at the C-vs-E step.

    Value vs. yes/no questions

    • Value question ("What is x?"): sufficient means exactly one possible value. Two different possible values = not sufficient.
    • Yes/no question ("Is x > 5?"): sufficient means the answer is always yes or always no. "Sometimes yes, sometimes no" = not sufficient — a definite NO is sufficient.

    The five classic DS traps

    1. C-trap: the answer looks like C but one statement alone suffices (recheck A/B/D before choosing C).
    2. Hidden constraints: "integer", "positive", "distinct" in the stem change everything — re-read the stem before evaluating each statement.
    3. Statement 2 repeats Statement 1: identical information → the answer is A/B/D, never C.
    4. Assuming too much: variables aren't integers/positive unless stated.
    5. Solving fully: you only need sufficiency — stop the algebra the moment uniqueness is clear.

    7. Data Insights: Graphics Interpretation

    Two-statement dropdown questions on charts and graphs: read the axes before the question.

    Graphics Interpretation presents one chart or graph (bar, line, scatter, bubble, box plot) with two related statements, each completed from a dropdown menu (3–5 options). Both statements share the one graphic, and both must be answered — there is no partial credit.

    Process

    1. Read the graphic before the statements: title, x-axis, y-axis (units and scale!), legend, footnotes. Half of GI errors come from misreading a scale or unit.
    2. Paraphrase each statement as a concrete lookup ("find the year where the two lines cross").
    3. Estimate first, calculate only if two dropdown options are close. The dropdown options are usually spaced enough for visual estimation.
    4. Watch for units: "in thousands", "percent vs. percentage points", dual axes with different scales.

    Chart-specific tactics

    • Line graphs: slope = rate of change; crossing lines = equal values. Don't confuse a steep line (fast change) with a high line (large value).
    • Scatter plots: trend direction = correlation; outliers are usually the point of the question. A trend line lets you estimate values between points.
    • Stacked bars: total = whole bar; segment = part. Read the legend — segments are often misattributed.
    • Bubble charts: three dimensions — x, y, and bubble size. Check what size encodes before comparing.

    Calculator: GI is in Data Insights, so the on-screen calculator is available — but reaching for it on every question burns your 45 minutes. Use it only when the dropdown options are numerically close.

    8. Data Insights: Table Analysis

    Sortable spreadsheet tables with three true/false-style statements — filter, don't eyeball.

    Table Analysis shows a sortable table (like a spreadsheet, usually 5–10 columns × 8–15 rows) plus three statements, each answered True/False or Yes/No from a dropdown. All three must be answered; no partial credit. The table can be sorted by clicking column headers — this is the whole game.

    Process

    1. Read the intro text first — it defines every column and unit. Column headers alone are often ambiguous.
    2. For each statement, identify the 1–2 columns that matter, sort by them, and read off the answer. Never scan an unsorted table by eye.
    3. Convert units before comparing (e.g. revenue in $ millions vs. cost in $ thousands).
    4. "True" means must be true given the table — if a statement could be false, it's False.

    Common task patterns

    • Extremes: "the largest", "the smallest" — sort the column, read the top/bottom row.
    • Thresholds: "more than X", "at least Y" — sort, find the cutoff, count.
    • Ratios computed from two columns: sort by the more variable column first; compute only for the rows near the cutoff.
    • Consistency checks: "in every year" — sort by year, scan the column for a counterexample; one counterexample makes it False.

    Time discipline: three statements on one table should take ~4 minutes total. If a statement needs a long multi-step computation, it's usually designed to be answered by sorting and elimination instead.

    9. Data Insights: Multi-Source Reasoning

    Two to three tabs of text, tables and charts — integrate across sources like a detective.

    Multi-Source Reasoning gives you 2–3 tabs (short texts, tables, charts, emails, memos) and several questions that require combining information across tabs. It's the most reading-heavy DI type — treat it like Reading Comprehension with numbers.

    Process

    1. Skim all tabs first (30–45 seconds): what is each tab — background memo? data table? proposal? Map each tab to one sentence.
    2. Read the question, then go back to the relevant tab(s). Never deep-read everything up front.
    3. For each answer choice, ask: "which tab proves or kills this?" Eliminate choices contradicted by any tab.
    4. Track who says what: in MSR, different tabs often present different people's claims. A statement true for one source may be disputed by another.

    Question flavours

    • Discrete: one question, one lookup — fastest; do these first.
    • True/false sets: three statements across tabs — verify each independently.
    • Inference: "which can be inferred" — must follow from the tabs combined; don't import outside knowledge.
    • Reconciliation: two tabs seem to conflict — find the distinction (different years, different definitions, different groups).

    Definition traps: MSR tabs often define terms slightly differently (e.g. "profit" vs. "operating profit"). When two tabs disagree, check definitions before concluding a contradiction — the test is checking whether you noticed.

    10. Two-Part Analysis & Question-Type Tactics

    The hybrid type: two linked answers, quant-flavoured or verbal-flavoured, one grid.

    Two-Part Analysis presents a scenario with two related questions answered in columns — e.g. pick the value of x in column 1 and the value of y in column 2, from the same option list. Both columns must be right; no partial credit. Content can be pure quant, pure verbal, or a hybrid.

    Quant-flavoured two-part

    • The two parts are usually linked by one equation system: solve the system once, read off both answers.
    • Sometimes the second column depends on the first — solve in dependency order, not column order.
    • When options are numbers, backsolve: test option pairs against the constraints.

    Verbal-flavoured two-part

    • Common frame: "identify the assumption AND the statement that most weakens it" — do the CR analysis first, then each column is one CR question.
    • Boldface-style two-part: column 1 = role of statement 1, column 2 = role of statement 2. Analyse each statement's role independently.

    Cross-type DI tactics (all five types)

    • Dropdown discipline: in GI/TA/MSR dropdowns, answer the statement you can verify fastest first — confidence compounds.
    • No partial credit anywhere in DI: a half-solved GI pair or TA triple scores zero. If you're stuck on one statement of a set, make your best elimination-based guess rather than leaving it.
    • Calculator triage: available in all of DI — use it for 3+ digit multiplication/division, never for things you can estimate.
    • Flag and move: DI questions vary wildly in time cost; a brutal MSR set can eat 6 minutes. Bookmark it, finish the cheaper questions, return with fresh eyes.

    11. Pacing, Guessing & Review Strategy

    2:08 per quant question, answer-changing rules, and the math of educated guessing.

    GMAT Focus gives you 45 minutes per section: ~2:08 per Quant question, ~1:57 per Verbal question, ~2:15 per DI question. The adaptive engine plus answer-review makes pacing strategy different from the old GMAT.

    Pacing benchmarks

    • Quant (21 Q / 45 min): 7 questions done by ~15:00, 14 by ~30:00. If you're behind by 3+ minutes at the halfway mark, speed up on the next five.
    • Verbal (23 Q / 45 min): read RC passages once — re-reading is the #1 time sink. Cap any single question at 3 minutes.
    • DI (20 Q / 45 min): sets (GI/TA/MSR) come in bundles; budget ~2:00 per question but up to 6:00 per set. Don't start a 3-tab MSR set with 4 minutes left.

    The review screen: change up to 3 answers per section

    • Only change an answer if you can name the specific error (misread, arithmetic slip, missed "not"). "Gut feeling" changes are statistically neutral-to-negative.
    • Bookmark liberally during the section (one click), then triage at review: fix clear errors first; leave 50/50s alone unless time is ample.
    • Never end a section with unanswered questions — there is no penalty for wrong answers, only for blanks (blanks hurt the adaptive estimate more).

    Guessing math

    • Eliminate even one choice and your odds jump from 20% to 25%. Two eliminations → 33%.
    • Guess and move on beats stall and sink: 4 minutes on one question starves two later questions the adaptive engine could have used to raise your score.
    • Pick a letter of the day for pure guesses to avoid decision fatigue — but always eliminate first.

    Error log: after every practice set, log each miss as: concept gap, misread, time pressure, or careless. If >30% of misses are misreads, your fix is process (slow down 10 seconds per question), not content.

    12. Test-Day Checklist & Score Reporting

    What to bring, the check-in gauntlet, and how your 205–805 gets to business schools.

    Test day is logistics plus nerves. The exam is offered at Pearson VUE test centres and online (with a human proctor). Registration, rescheduling and score sending all happen at mba.com.

    Before the day

    • ID: one government-issued photo ID with signature, name matching your registration exactly (check middle names). Digital IDs are not accepted at most centres.
    • Confirm your appointment, centre address and reporting time 48 hours ahead; plan to arrive 30 minutes early.
    • Online test: run the system check days ahead — wired internet, quiet room, clear desk, no headphones. The whiteboard (physical or online) replaces scratch paper.
    • Sleep and food beat last-minute cramming: do light review the day before, nothing new.

    At the centre

    • Expect palm-vein scan, photo, and locker for all belongings — phones and watches are banned from the room.
    • You get an optional 10-minute break between sections (the 2:15 clock excludes it). Use it: water, snack, reset.
    • Choose your section order at the start — most test-takers lead with their strongest section to bank confidence.
    • Scratch booklet/whiteboard provided; raise your hand for a replacement, don't wait until it's full.

    Scores and reporting

    • Unofficial scores appear immediately after the test; the Official Score Report arrives within 3–5 days (up to 20 in rare review cases).
    • Your report shows the total (205–805) plus section scores (60–90) with percentiles, and an Enhanced Score Report breaks down performance by question type, timing and difficulty.
    • You choose after seeing your score whether to keep or cancel it — and cancelled scores can later be reinstated (for a fee).
    • Score sending: select programs in your mba.com account; each additional report beyond the included ones costs extra. Scores stay valid 5 years.
    • Retakes: 16 days minimum between attempts, max 5 in 12 months, 8 lifetime. Schools see only the scores you send (plus any you chose to report).

    CR (50)

    CRStrengthen = support — A strengthener makes the conclusion MORE likely — new evidence, no new assumptions needed.
    CRWeaken = attack — A weakener breaks the evidence→conclusion link, often by offering an alternative cause.
    CRAssumption = missing link — The assumption is unstated and NECESSARY: negate it, and the argument collapses (negation test).
    CRNegation test — To test an assumption choice: negate it. If the argument falls apart, it's the assumption.
    CRInference = must be true — An inference MUST follow from the premises — never choose what 'could' be true.
    CRExtreme language — In inference questions, answers with 'all', 'never', 'always' are usually wrong.
    CRCorrelation ≠ causation — Two trends together don't prove one causes the other — look for the third factor.
    CRAlternative cause — The most common weakener: something else caused the observed effect.
    CRRepresentative sample — Surveys and studies assume the sample represents the whole — attack the sample to weaken.
    CRScope shift — Watch for conclusion words not in the evidence — the gap between them is the assumption.
    CRParadox resolution — Resolve paradoxes with a fact that lets BOTH surprising statements be true.
    CRBoldface roles — Identify each boldface part: premise, conclusion, counter-premise, or background.
    CRMain conclusion — The conclusion is what the author wants you to believe — often signaled by 'thus', 'therefore', 'should'.
    CRIntermediate conclusion — Some arguments have a sub-conclusion supporting the main one — don't confuse them.
    CREvaluate the argument — An 'evaluate' answer is useful if different responses would change your verdict on the conclusion.
    CRMethod of reasoning — Describe WHAT the author did (e.g., analogy, counterexample), not whether it's good.
    CRFlaw = describe the error — Name the logical mistake: e.g., confusing correlation with causation, part-to-whole.
    CRPart-to-whole flaw — What's true of a part need not be true of the whole (and vice versa).
    CREquivocation flaw — Using one word in two senses within an argument is the equivocation flaw.
    CRCircular reasoning — An argument that assumes its conclusion in the premises begs the question.
    CRAnalogy strength — Analogies hold only if the two cases are similar in RELEVANT respects.
    CRCausal claims need — To prove cause: correlation + no alternative cause + right time order.
    CRTime-order trap — The cause must precede the effect — reversed timing kills causal claims.
    CRNumbers vs rates — Rising numbers can hide falling rates (and vice versa) — check the denominator.
    CRPercent vs absolute — 'Doubled' sounds big; check the base — 2 to 4 is +100% but tiny.
    CRSurvivorship bias — Studying only survivors (successful firms, planes that returned) distorts conclusions.
    CRSelf-selection — Voluntary survey respondents differ from non-respondents — results skew.
    CRPlacebo comparison — A treatment 'works' only versus a proper control group.
    CRBefore/after trap — Improvement after an intervention may be regression to the mean, not the treatment.
    CRNecessary vs sufficient — Necessary: must hold for conclusion. Sufficient: guarantees it. Don't confuse them.
    CROnly/Unless logic — 'Only A if B' = A requires B. 'Unless' = if-not.
    CRContrapositive — 'If P then Q' is equivalent to 'If not Q then not P' — use it to test inferences.
    CRSome = at least one — In logic, 'some' means ≥1 and doesn't rule out 'all'.
    CRMost = more than half — 'Most' means >50%, a stronger claim than 'some'.
    CRCannot weaken with — An answer that SUPPORTS the conclusion can never be the weakener — eliminate first.
    CROut-of-scope answers — If an answer introduces entirely new topics, it's probably wrong.
    CRAnswer the question asked — A true statement that doesn't address the conclusion is a trap.
    CRPredict before reading — Form your own answer from the stimulus, then match — don't let choices seduce you.
    CRWeaken the plan — To weaken a proposal: show it won't achieve its goal or its costs outweigh benefits.
    CRStrengthen the plan — To strengthen: show the mechanism works or remove an objection.
    CRPrinciple questions — Match the SITUATION to the principle — the principle must cover the case exactly.
    CRParallel reasoning — Match the logical STRUCTURE (flaw included), not the topic.
    CRFill in the blank — The blank must complete the argument's logic — usually the conclusion or key premise.
    CRExcept questions — For 'EXCEPT': four choices do the job, one doesn't — find the odd one out.
    CRDegree words matter — 'Some' vs 'most' vs 'all' change everything in inference and assumption.
    CRDon't bring outside knowledge — Judge only by the stimulus — real-world facts can mislead.
    CRConclusion indicators — Thus, therefore, hence, so, consequently → conclusion usually follows.
    CRPremise indicators — Because, since, given that, for → evidence usually follows.
    CRCausal weakener template — Find: reverse causation, alternative cause, or coincidence.
    CRCausal strengthener template — Find: no alternative cause, or the effect disappears without the cause.

    DIScatterplot reading — Each dot is one observation; trend direction and spread are what questions test.
    DIBubble size — In bubble charts, area (not diameter) encodes the third variable.
    DIDual axes — Two y-axes can have different scales — compare within a series, not across.
    DIFootnote gold — 'In thousands', 'seasonally adjusted' — footnotes change every number's meaning.
    DIMSR tab labels — Tab names tell you where to look — 'Email', 'Table', 'Chart'. Go straight there.
