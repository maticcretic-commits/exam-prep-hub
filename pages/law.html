Study Material

English: read a passage as an argument

Map the passage in four moves

Issue: what question is being discussed? Position: what does the author want you to accept? Support: which reasons or examples carry that position? Limit: where does the author qualify the claim?

Main idea vs. topic

The topic may be “data privacy”; the main idea is the author’s specific claim about it. A correct main-idea answer covers the whole passage without being broader or narrower than the text.

Inference rule

An inference must be supported, not merely possible. Reject options that add a new cause, universalise a limited claim, reverse a relationship, or treat an example as the conclusion.

Tone words

Critical points out defects; sceptical doubts a claim; qualified accepts with limits; analytical examines parts; polemical argues aggressively. Pick the mildest word fully supported.

Vocabulary in context

Replace the word with each option and preserve the sentence’s logic and emotional force. The familiar dictionary meaning can be wrong in context.

Option-elimination traps

Watch for always/never, distorted comparisons, true facts not stated in the passage, half-right combinations, and answers that describe evidence rather than the conclusion.

Worked example. “The policy lowered delays in urban courts, though rural courts lacked stable internet. A hybrid model is therefore preferable.” The conclusion is not “online courts work”; it is the qualified recommendation for a hybrid model.
Legal reasoning: apply the given rule, not personal opinion

IRAC in one minute

Issue: the legal question. Rule: the principle supplied. Application: match each fact to each condition. Conclusion: choose the result that follows with the fewest assumptions.

Constitution essentials

Article 14: equality before law and equal protection. Article 19: six core freedoms, subject to reasonable restrictions. Article 21: life and personal liberty under fair, just and reasonable procedure. Article 32: Supreme Court remedies for fundamental rights.

Five constitutional writs

Habeas corpus: produce a detained person. Mandamus: command public duty. Prohibition: stop a lower body before excess jurisdiction. Certiorari: quash its order. Quo warranto: test authority to hold public office.

Contract basics

A contract usually requires offer, acceptance, lawful consideration, competent parties, free consent and lawful object. A proposal becomes a promise when accepted. Coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation can make consent defective.

Tort essentials

Negligence asks for duty, breach, causation and damage. Defamation concerns a false statement harming reputation and communicated to a third party. Vicarious liability may make an employer answer for an employee acting in the course of employment.

Crime basics

Most offences combine a prohibited act (actus reus) with a blameworthy mental state (mens rea). Intention, knowledge, recklessness and negligence are different states; use only the one defined in the passage.

Stare decisisFollow binding precedent.
Audi alteram partemHear the other side.
Nemo judex in causa suaNo one judges their own cause.
Volenti non fit injuriaConsent can defeat a claim for injury within the accepted risk.
Res ipsa loquiturThe occurrence itself may indicate negligence.
Ignorantia juris non excusatIgnorance of law is no excuse.
Ratio decidendiThe binding legal reason in a judgment.
Obiter dictaJudicial remarks that do not bind.
Caveat emptorBuyer beware: check before you buy.
Locus standiStanding to bring a legal action.
Ex post factoDone after the event; retrospective.
Method. Underline every condition in the stated principle. Mark each as met, not met or uncertain. If one necessary condition fails, liability usually fails—even if the outcome feels unfair.
Logical reasoning: see the skeleton of an argument

Premise and conclusion

Premises are offered as reasons; the conclusion is the claim they support. Indicators such as “because” often introduce reasons, while “therefore”, “thus” and “so” often introduce conclusions.

Assumption

An assumption is an unstated bridge the argument needs. Use the negation test: negate an option; if the argument collapses, that option was necessary.

Strengthen and weaken

A strengthener closes a gap, supports a causal link or rules out an alternative. A weakener opens a gap, supplies an alternative cause, questions the sample or shows the proposed step will not produce the result.

Causation traps

Correlation is not automatically causation. Test reverse causation, a common third cause, coincidence, selection bias and whether the cause precedes the effect.

Necessary vs sufficient

If A is sufficient for B: A → B. You may infer not-B → not-A (contrapositive), but not B → A (converse). “Only if” introduces a necessary condition; “if” introduces a sufficient one.

Syllogism discipline

“All A are B” does not mean “all B are A.” “Some” means at least one. From “some A are B” and “some B are C,” no A–C overlap is guaranteed.

Worked example. “Every successful petition met the filing deadline. This petition missed the deadline.” Valid result: it was not successful (contrapositive). You cannot conclude that every timely petition succeeds.
Quantitative techniques: the compact formula sheet

Percentages

Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100. A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall gives 0.8 × 1.2 = 0.96, a net 4% fall—not zero.

New value = Original × (1 ± rate/100)

Ratio and proportion

If A:B = 3:5 and total = 64, there are 8 equal parts; A = 24 and B = 40. To combine ratios, equalise the common term first.

Share = Total × own parts ÷ total parts

Average

Average = total ÷ count. When one value is replaced, new total = old total − removed value + added value. Weighted average respects each group’s size.

Combined average = (n₁a₁+n₂a₂) ÷ (n₁+n₂)

Profit, loss and discount

Profit or loss percentage uses cost price. Discount uses marked price. Successive discounts multiply; 10% then 20% means paying 0.9 × 0.8 = 72%, a 28% discount.

Profit% = Profit ÷ Cost Price × 100

Simple interest

Simple interest grows only on principal. Compound interest grows on principal plus accumulated interest.

SI = P × R × T ÷ 100

Data interpretation

Read title, unit and base year first. Estimate before calculating. Compare like with like, and check whether a chart shows absolute numbers, percentages or percentage-point changes.

Worked example. A batch rises from 240 to 300 students. Change = 60; percentage rise = 60 ÷ 240 × 100 = 25%.
Current affairs & static GK: make every event answerable

The six-box note

For each major event capture: what happened, who is involved, where, when, why it matters, and the legal or institutional link. Add no more than five bullets.

Constitution structure

Parliament consists of the President, Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. The Supreme Court is the apex court. Fundamental Rights are in Part III; Directive Principles in Part IV; Fundamental Duties in Part IVA.

Key constitutional offices

Know the appointment, tenure and function of the President, Prime Minister, Chief Justice of India, Attorney General, Comptroller and Auditor General, Election Commission and Finance Commission.

Legislation tracker

Distinguish Bill from Act; ordinary Bill from Money Bill; ordinance from statute; committee recommendation from binding law. Track the stage, not just the headline.

Judgment tracker

Record court, bench, legal issue, holding and effect. Separate what the court actually decided from observations and media summaries.

International affairs

For organisations, learn mandate, members, headquarters and the event linking it to the news. For treaties, learn purpose, parties and whether India signed, ratified or merely discussed it.

Revision loop. Same day: write. After 24 hours: recall without notes. After 7 days: answer five questions. After 30 days: compress the month into one sheet. This page is a method guide, not a live news feed; your exam-window current affairs must be updated from dated reporting.
Mini practice set: six explained drills

  1. English

    Question: A passage concedes that a reform is expensive but argues that its long-term savings exceed the initial cost. The author’s stance is best described as? Answer: Qualified support. Why: The cost is acknowledged, but the reform is endorsed overall.

  2. Legal

    Rule: Consent obtained by threatening unlawful detention is not free. X threatens to lock Y in a room unless Y signs. Answer: Consent is not free. Why: The stated threat matches the rule; outside moral judgments are unnecessary.

  3. Logic

    Argument: “The city added buses and congestion fell; therefore buses caused the fall.” Weakener: A major bridge also reopened the same week. Why: It supplies a plausible alternative cause.

  4. Quant

    Question: A price of ₹800 is discounted 15%. Answer: ₹680. Why: Discount = 800 × .15 = 120; selling price = 800 − 120.

  5. Syllogism

    Premises: All judges are lawyers; some lawyers are teachers. Conclusion: Some judges are teachers? Answer: Not necessarily. Why: The “some lawyers” group may contain no judges.

  6. Current affairs

    Question: A Bill has passed Lok Sabha but awaits Rajya Sabha consideration. Is it an Act? Answer: No. Why: Passage by one House alone does not complete the ordinary legislative process.

  7. Contract

    Rule: A proposal becomes a promise when accepted. X emails an offer to sell; Y replies “agreed”. Answer: A promise exists. Why: Offer plus acceptance completes the agreement; the medium does not change the rule.

  8. Data reading

    Question: Quarterly sales are 120, 150 and 180 units. What is the average? Answer: 150. Why: (120 + 150 + 180) ÷ 3 = 150.

AILET’s 70-question logical reasoning: play the format

Direct questions, not long passages

AILET leans toward direct argument questions rather than CLAT-style long passages. Answer in one pass where the structure is clear; flag and return only on long stimuli.

Volume discipline

70 questions in 120 minutes alongside English and GK means roughly 90 seconds per reasoning question. Train with 20-question timed sets before attempting full mocks.

Same core skills

Assumption, strengthen/weaken, inference, syllogism and flaw questions transfer directly between the two papers. Prepare them once, practise them in both formats.

Legal knowledge inside logic

AILET may embed legal principles inside logical-reasoning questions. Apply the stated principle mechanically, as in the Legal reasoning chapter — no prior law study needed.

Legal current-affairs themes for the 2027 cycle

New criminal laws

The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam replaced the IPC, CrPC and Indian Evidence Act. Expect questions on the renaming, new offences and procedure changes.

Constitutional developments

Track amendments, landmark Supreme Court and High Court judgments, and judicial appointments — who decided what, on which issue, with what holding.

Legislation and institutions

New Acts, Bills at each legislative stage, ordinances, and the mandate of commissions, tribunals and international courts that appear in the news.

Study Plan

Weeks 1–2

Baseline + foundations

Take one untimed diagnostic. Build daily reading, argument basics, arithmetic revision and legal-principle drills. Start one-page current-affairs briefs.

Weeks 3–4

Section control

Complete 3 timed sectional tests per week. Review every option—not only the right answer. Create a compact error log by category.

Weeks 5–6

Full-paper transition

Take 2 CLAT and 1 AILET mock each week. Fix an order of sections, but test one alternative before committing.

Weeks 7–8

Volume + selection

Move to 4 full mocks per week. Practise leaving low-confidence questions. Revise monthly current affairs through recall, not rereading.

Week 9

Exam simulation

Match the 2–4 PM exam slot. Use paper-like conditions. After each mock, make a 3-point correction plan for the next one.

Week 10

Taper, revise, protect accuracy

Reduce new material. Revisit errors, formulas and current-affairs briefs. Check admit-card instructions and sleep on schedule.
