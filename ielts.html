    Test Format Decoded: The Four Sections

    Both versions

    IELTS tests four skills — Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking — and takes about 2 hours 45 minutes in total (plus Speaking, which may be scheduled on a different day). Every section is scored on the 0–9 band scale, and your overall band is the average of the four section bands, rounded to the nearest half band.

    Listening — 40 questions, ~30 minutes (+ transfer time)

    Four recordings, each with 10 questions: Part 1 is an everyday conversation between two people (e.g. booking accommodation); Part 2 is an everyday monologue (e.g. a talk about local facilities); Part 3 is an academic discussion with 2–4 speakers (e.g. students discussing an assignment); Part 4 is an academic monologue (e.g. a lecture). You hear each recording only once. On paper-based IELTS you get 10 minutes to transfer answers; on computer-delivered IELTS you get about 2 minutes to check. Spelling counts — a correctly heard but misspelled answer scores zero. One mark per question, no negative marking.

    Reading — 40 questions, 60 minutes (no transfer time)

    Academic: three long passages from books, journals and magazines, in increasing difficulty. General Training: three sections — short everyday texts (notices, adverts), workplace texts, then one longer general-interest text. There is no extra time to transfer answers — write them on the answer sheet as you go. Budget roughly 20 minutes per passage.

    Writing — 2 tasks, 60 minutes (you manage the clock)

    Task 1: at least 150 words, recommended ~20 minutes. Task 2: at least 250 words, recommended ~40 minutes. Task 2 counts double — it carries two-thirds of your Writing band. Under-length responses are penalised under Task Achievement/Response.

    Speaking — 3 parts, 11–14 minutes

    A face-to-face (or video-call) interview with a trained examiner, recorded for quality control. Part 1: interview on familiar topics (4–5 min). Part 2: cue card, 1 minute to prepare, then speak for 1–2 minutes (3–4 min total). Part 3: discussion of abstract ideas linked to the Part 2 topic (4–5 min). It is identical for Academic and General Training.

    Paper vs computer

    The content, timing, question types and scoring are the same. Differences are practical: computer Listening gives ~2 minutes to check answers instead of 10; typing vs handwriting for Writing; on-screen highlighting instead of underlining. Choose whichever you perform better on — results come in 3–5 days for computer, about 13 days for paper.

    Academic vs General Training: Which Test, What Differs

    Both versions

    The one-sentence rule

    Listening and Speaking are identical in both versions — same recordings, same interview, same scoring. Only Reading and Writing differ. Everything else (fee, 0–9 scale, 2-year score validity, test-day procedure) is the same.

    Choose by purpose

    Academic: university admission (undergraduate and postgraduate), professional registration (doctors, nurses, engineers). Universities almost always require Academic. General Training: migration and permanent residency (Canada PR requires GT only; Australia accepts either), work visas, and training below degree level. Book the wrong version and you pay the fee again — check what your institution or immigration body demands before booking.

    Reading differences

    Academic Reading: three long academic passages (science, history, society, technology), 40 questions, increasing difficulty — tests critical analysis, argument structure, implicit meaning. GT Reading: three sections with 5–6 shorter texts — Section 1: everyday survival texts (notices, adverts, timetables); Section 2: workplace texts (job descriptions, manuals); Section 3: one longer general-interest article. Same 60 minutes, same 40 questions, but the GT texts use everyday vocabulary and test practical comprehension.

    Writing differences

    Academic Task 1: describe visual data — line/bar graphs, pie charts, tables, process diagrams, maps — in 150+ words, formal objective style, no opinion. GT Task 1: write a letter — formal, semi-formal or informal depending on the prompt — covering all bullet points in 150+ words. Task 2 is the same format for both: a 250+ word essay responding to a point of view, argument or problem. (GT topics lean slightly more general/personal; Academic leans slightly more abstract.)

    Scoring differences

    Same 0–9 descriptors for Writing and Speaking. For Reading, the raw-score-to-band conversion differs slightly: because GT texts are easier, you need more correct answers for the same band (roughly: Academic 30/40 ≈ Band 7, GT needs about 34/40 ≈ Band 7 — exact tables vary slightly by test). Listening conversion is identical.

    Other variants

    IELTS for UKVI uses the same content with extra security protocols for UK visa categories. IELTS Online lets you take the test from home (Academic only, where available) with a human-proctored Speaking test. IELTS One Skill Retake (computer-delivered only) lets you redo one section within 60 days of your original test — check whether your target institution accepts it.

    Listening: 40 Questions, One Hearing

    Both versions

    Know the four parts

    Part 1 (easiest): everyday conversation, two speakers — form completion, personal details, numbers, spellings. Part 2: everyday monologue — maps/plans, matching, multiple choice. Part 3 (hardest conversation): academic discussion, 2–4 speakers — opinions, attitudes, matching speakers to ideas. Part 4 (hardest): academic lecture, one speaker — note/summary/flow-chart completion. Difficulty rises; concentration must not drop in Part 4.

    Use the reading time — predict before you listen

    You get time to read questions before each part. Underline keywords, predict the word type (noun? verb? number? date?), and think of paraphrases — the recording will reword the question. If the question says "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER", obey it exactly: "60 local craftsmen" is wrong if the limit is two words.

    Question-type tactics

    Form/note/table/flow-chart/summary completion: answers follow the order of the recording; predict grammar (plural? tense?) from the sentence stem. Multiple choice: distil each option to its core idea before listening; speakers mention several options but only the final stance is correct — watch for signposts like "however", "but", "actually". Matching: cross out used options as you hear matches. Map/plan labelling: find the start point fast (entrance, north arrow), trace with your pencil, listen for directional language (opposite, adjacent to, just past); if lost, re-anchor at the next clear landmark.

    Traps that cost bands

    Changed answers: the speaker says one thing then corrects it ("...on the right — sorry, on the left"). The correction is the answer. Similar words, wrong answer: distractors echo question words but answer a different question. Plurals: "book" vs "books" — listen for the /s/. Spelling: names and unusual words are spelled out; write them letter-perfect. Word limits: extra words invalidate an otherwise correct answer.

    Answer-sheet discipline

    Paper: use the 10-minute transfer to check spelling, plurals, and word limits — never leave blanks (no negative marking, so guess). Computer: answers go straight in; use the ~2-minute check the same way. Write clearly; if the examiner cannot read it, it is wrong.

    Reading: 60 Minutes, 40 Questions, Zero Transfer Time

    Both versions

    The golden rule: questions first, passage second

    Never read a whole passage top-to-bottom first — you do not have time. Read the questions, underline keywords (names, dates, numbers, technical terms), then hunt. Roughly 20 minutes per passage; if a question resists for 2 minutes, move on and return later. Unanswered questions score zero — guess at the end if needed.

    Skim, then scan

    Skim (1–2 min): title, first and last sentences of paragraphs, any headings — build a mental map of where topics live. Scan: sweep for question keywords and their paraphrases — IELTS almost never repeats the question's wording in the passage. Once located, read 2–3 sentences around the keyword carefully before answering.

    True / False / Not Given (the band-killer)

    TRUE: the passage states the same meaning. FALSE: the passage contradicts it. NOT GIVEN: the information is simply absent — do not infer. Traps: qualifying words ("all", "always", "never", "only") flip TRUE to FALSE; a detail that is mentioned but not compared the way the statement claims → NOT GIVEN. Never use outside knowledge — the passage is the only truth.

    Matching headings

    Match the paragraph's main idea, not a single striking detail. Read first and last sentences, ignore examples (they support, never define). One heading per paragraph; distractors share vocabulary with the paragraph but describe a different idea. Eliminate used headings as you go.

    Other types, fast

    Multiple choice: eliminate two obviously wrong options first. Matching information/features: note which paragraph each fact lives in while skimming. Summary/note/table/sentence completion: respect word limits; copy words exactly as spelled in the passage; check grammar fit (a/an, singular/plural). Matching sentence endings: grammar-match the halves first, then verify meaning.

    GT readers, note

    Sections 1–2 are fast factual texts — bank time here (aim 12–15 min each) so Section 3's longer article gets a full 25–30 minutes. Academic readers: passage 3 is the hardest — if you are behind, secure passages 1–2 first.

    Writing Task 1 (Academic): Describe the Visual

    Academic

    What the examiner wants (4-paragraph structure)

    Paragraph 1 — Introduction: paraphrase the prompt in one or two sentences ("The bar chart illustrates…"). Paragraph 2 — Overview: 2–3 key trends, no numbers ("Overall, …"). Paragraphs 3–4 — Details: grouped data with figures. Never give opinions, never write a conclusion ("In conclusion" belongs in Task 2, not here). Under 150 words = automatic Task Achievement penalty.

    Static vs dynamic visuals

    Dynamic (changes over time — line graphs, bar charts with years): use trend language — rose, surged, plummeted, fluctuated, levelled off — plus degree adverbs (sharply, gradually, marginally). Static (one point in time — pie charts, tables without years): use comparison language — higher than, the largest proportion, accounted for, in contrast. Matching the language to the visual type is a fast Lexical Resource win.

    Trend vocabulary bank

    Up: increase, rise, climb, surge, soar, peak (at). Down: decrease, fall, decline, drop, plunge, plummet, hit a low of. Flat: remain stable, level off, plateau, hold steady. Change nouns: a rise/increase/fall/drop/fluctuation. Always vary: "rose sharply" then "saw a sharp rise" — noun↔verb swaps double your range without new words.

    Process diagrams and maps

    Processes: passive voice dominates ("The coffee beans are roasted, then they are ground"); sequence with first, subsequently, following this, finally. State the overall purpose in the overview. Maps: describe location and change — "to the north of", "adjacent to", "was demolished and replaced by"; past tense for old features, present/past for current ones. Group changes logically (e.g. north side vs south side).

    Band-7+ habits

    Select, don't list: group similar trends and report only key features — mechanical listing of every number reads as Band 5–6. Compare within sentences ("twice as many as", "while X fell, Y rose"). Proofread the last 2 minutes: articles (a/the), subject-verb agreement, and data accuracy. Aim 160–180 words — more invites errors.

    Writing Task 1 (General Training): The Letter

    General Training

    Step 1: identify the letter type

    The prompt tells you who you are writing to — that fixes the tone. Formal (someone you don't know: a manager, a company, an official): "Dear Sir or Madam" → "Yours faithfully". Semi-formal (someone you know slightly: a landlord, a neighbour, a colleague): "Dear Mr Brown" → "Yours sincerely". Informal (a friend): "Dear Anita" → "Best wishes" / "Warm wishes". Mismatching greeting and tone is an instant Task Achievement hit.

    Formal vs informal language

    Formal: longer sentences, passive voice ("The broken light needs to be fixed"), modals (could, would), no contractions ("cannot", not "can't"), formal vocabulary (request not ask, purchase not buy, respond not write back, at your earliest convenience not when you are free). Informal: conversational, contractions fine, direct speech ("Can you fix the light so I don't have an accident?"), warmer closings. Semi-formal sits between: polite but not stiff.

    Cover every bullet point

    The prompt gives 3–4 bullet points — each must be answered with detail, not one flat sentence. One sentence per bullet is not enough; add a sentence of explanation or consequence to each. Common letter purposes: complaining, requesting information, applying for a job, apologising, inviting, giving advice, making suggestions, explaining a situation.

    Structure (150+ words, ~20 minutes)

    Paragraph 1: state who you are and why you are writing ("I am writing to complain about…"). Paragraphs 2–3: work through the bullet points, one idea per paragraph. Final paragraph: say what you want to happen next ("I would be grateful if you could…") and close with the matching sign-off. Do not add addresses or dates — they waste time and words.

    Band-7+ habits

    Open with a clear purpose sentence; close with a clear expected action. Vary sentence length — one short punchy sentence among longer ones reads naturally. In formal letters, conditionals shine ("Should you require further information, please…"). Proofread for tone slips: a single "gonna" or "can't" in a formal letter is a Lexical Resource wound.

    Writing Task 2: The Essay That Counts Double

    Both versions

    Why Task 2 matters most

    Task 2 is worth two-thirds of your Writing band (Task 1 is one-third). It needs 250+ words in ~40 minutes. The four criteria — Task Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy — are weighted equally, and the final Writing band averages Task 1 and Task 2 with Task 2 doubled.

    The five question types (identify before you write)

    1. Opinion ("To what extent do you agree or disagree?"): pick ONE side and defend it throughout — a wandering middle costs Task Response. 2. Discussion ("Discuss both views and give your opinion"): one body paragraph per view, your opinion in the introduction and conclusion. 3. Advantages/disadvantages: balanced body paragraphs; if asked "do the advantages outweigh…", state your verdict clearly. 4. Problem/solution ("What are the causes? What can be done?"): causes paragraph, solutions paragraph — answer both questions. 5. Two-part question: answer both questions directly, one per body paragraph. Answering the wrong type is the most expensive mistake in Task 2.

    The 4-paragraph engine

    Introduction (2–3 sentences): paraphrase the question + thesis (your position). Body 1: topic sentence → explain → example → link. Body 2: same skeleton. Conclusion (2 sentences): restate your position, no new ideas. Aim 270–300 words: safely over 250 without inviting errors.

    Band 7 vs Band 6

    Band 6: addresses the task but ideas are underdeveloped; cohesive devices feel mechanical ("Firstly… Secondly… Moreover…"); adequate vocabulary with noticeable errors; mix of simple and complex sentences with errors. Band 7: fully addresses all parts; ideas extended and supported; cohesive devices used naturally; less common vocabulary with awareness of style and collocation; frequent error-free sentences. The jump is development + natural cohesion + accuracy, not fancier words.

    Ideas under pressure

    Generate ideas from five lenses: money/economy, health, education, environment, society/culture. Ask "who is affected and how?" Realistic examples beat invented statistics — examiners don't verify facts, they assess language. Plan 5 minutes, write 30, check 5.

    What never helps

    Memorised phrases ("In the modern contemporary era…") signal Band 5. Idioms don't belong in academic essays. Overlong essays (350+ words) multiply error chances. And never write fewer than 250 words — the Task Response penalty is automatic.

    Speaking: Three Parts, One Conversation

    Both versions

    How it is marked

    Four criteria, equally weighted, each 0–9, then averaged: Fluency & Coherence (speak at length, logically, without undue hesitation), Lexical Resource (range and precision of vocabulary), Grammatical Range & Accuracy (variety + correctness), Pronunciation (clarity and natural stress/intonation — accent is fine). There are no right answers: opinions are never scored, communication is.

    Part 1 — Interview (4–5 min)

    Familiar topics: home, work, studies, hobbies, daily life. Give 2–4 sentence answers — one-word replies starve the examiner of evidence; long monologues get you cut off. Extend naturally: answer + reason + example ("I cycle to work. It's faster than the bus, and last month I…").

    Part 2 — The long turn (3–4 min)

    You get a cue card with bullet points, 1 minute to prepare (paper and pencil provided), then speak for 1–2 minutes. Use the minute for keywords, not sentences. Cover every bullet, but develop each — a story beats a list. Structure: set the scene (who/what/when/where) → describe → explain why it matters. If you run dry, the examiner may prompt you; keep going until stopped — stopping at 50 seconds caps your Fluency evidence.

    Part 3 — Discussion (4–5 min)

    Abstract questions linked to the Part 2 topic ("Why do people enjoy travelling? How has tourism changed?"). Move from personal to general: opinions, comparisons, causes and effects, speculation about the future. Longer, more analytical answers are expected here. Use discourse markers naturally — however, as a result, on the whole — and hedge like a native ("It depends…", "Generally speaking…").

    Pronunciation truths

    Clarity beats accent imitation. Stress the right syllable (PHOtograph vs phoTOgraphy), chunk speech into sense groups, and let intonation rise and fall — monotone delivery drags the Pronunciation band down. Record yourself weekly; you will hear hesitations you never felt.

    Band-7+ habits

    Self-correct smoothly and move on (occasional correction is fine; stalling is not). Paraphrase the examiner's question instead of echoing it. Use a range of tenses — past for experiences, present for habits, future/conditional for speculation. Never memorise answers: examiners detect scripts instantly and penalise them under Fluency.

    Band Scores 0–9: How Marks Become Bands

    Both versions

    The scale

    Bands run 0–9 in half-band steps. Band 9 = expert user, Band 8 = very good, Band 7 = good, Band 6 = competent, Band 5 = modest, Band 4 = limited, Band 3 = extremely limited, Band 2 = intermittent, Band 1 = non-user, Band 0 = did not attempt the test. Most universities ask for 6.5 or 7.0 overall, often with minimums per section; immigration bodies usually need specific minimums in each section.

    How each section is scored

    Listening and Reading: one mark per correct answer out of 40, then converted to a band — no half marks, no negative marking. Roughly: 23–26 correct ≈ Band 6, 27–29 ≈ 6.5, 30–32 ≈ 7, 33–34 ≈ 7.5, 35+ ≈ 8. (Exact tables shift slightly per test.) Writing and Speaking: examiners score four criteria each, then average. Writing criteria: Task Achievement (Task 1) / Task Response (Task 2), Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy. Speaking criteria: Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, Pronunciation. For Writing, Task 2 counts double.

    The rounding rules

    Overall band = average of the four section bands. An average ending in .25 rounds up to the next half band (6.25 → 6.5); ending in .75 rounds up to the next whole band (7.75 → 8.0). Averages ending in .125 or .625 round down (6.125 → 6.0; 6.625 → 6.5). Example: Listening 7.5, Reading 7.0, Writing 6.5, Speaking 6.5 = 27.5 ÷ 4 = 6.875 → rounds to 7.0.

    Strategy from the maths

    Because of rounding, one weak section often decides your band. If your three strongest average 7.5, a 6.0 in the fourth gives 6.625 → 6.5; lift that section to 6.5 and you get 6.75 → 7.0. Fix your weakest section first — every half band there is worth a full half band overall. Task 2 in Writing is the highest-leverage 40 minutes of your preparation.

    Re-marking (Enquiry on Results)

    If your score is close to a boundary you need, you can request an Enquiry on Results: a senior examiner re-marks your test and the score can go up, down, or stay. The fee is refunded if your score changes. You have 6 weeks from the test date; IELTS One Skill Retake (one section, computer-delivered, within 60 days) is a separate option — check your institution accepts it before choosing.

    Grammar for IELTS: The Errors That Cost Bands

    Both versions

    Accuracy is a scoring criterion — in Writing AND Speaking

    Grammatical Range and Accuracy counts in both productive skills. You don't need perfect English for Band 7, but you need frequent error-free sentences and visible variety: simple, compound, and complex sentences working together. These are the error families that appear most in Band 5–6 scripts.

    1. Subject–verb agreement

    "The number of tourists has risen" (singular verb — the subject is "number", not "tourists"). "Data" takes plural verbs in formal English ("The data show…"). Long subjects separate subject and verb: find the true head noun before choosing the verb. This error is invisible to the writer but glaring to the examiner.

    2. Articles (a / an / the / none)

    First mention → a/an ("a new policy"); later mentions → the ("the policy"). Unique or specified nouns → the ("the government", "the internet"). Plurals and uncountables in general statements → no article ("Pollution is rising", not "The pollution"). Master articles and your Lexical/Accuracy bands rise together.

    3. Tense control

    Task 1 visuals with years: past tense for finished periods ("exports rose between 2000 and 2010"), present for current/static facts. Processes and general truths: present simple ("Water boils…"). Speaking Part 2: past tense for past experiences — slipping into present mid-story is a classic Band-6 tell. Conditionals: "If governments invested more, pollution would fall" (second conditional for hypotheticals).

    4. Sentence variety

    Complex sentences: subordinate clauses (although, because, which, who), relative clauses ("the policy, which was introduced in 2020,…"), conditionals, passives ("The museum was built in 1990"). Variety is "range": three long accurate complex sentences beat ten short simple ones. But a wrong complex sentence is worse than a right simple one — attempt structures you can control.

    5. Plurals, prepositions, word form

    Uncountable nouns never take plurals: information, advice, research, equipment, evidence, traffic, furniture, knowledge — "much research", never "many researches". Prepositions pair with verbs/adjectives: depend on, differ from, agree with/on, responsible for, interested in, result in (cause) vs result from (effect). Word form: the question needs the right part of speech — "economy" (noun) vs "economic" (adjective) vs "economically" (adverb).

    6. Cohesion grammar

    Reference words must agree: "The government has introduced a new policy. It aims to…" (singular it). Parallel structure: "not only X but also Y" needs matching forms ("not only faster but also cheaper"). These small systems errors quietly cap Band 6.

    Vocabulary: Precision Beats Fancy Words

    Both versions

    What Lexical Resource actually rewards

    Range, precision, collocation (words that naturally go together), and awareness of style. Precision beats obscurity: the exact common word ("declined gradually") outscores a misused exotic one ("the statistics plummeted in a modicum fashion" — Band 5 territory). Never use a word you cannot control.

    Collocations (learn words in pairs)

    Native-like English is collocation: make a decision (not do), strong coffee (not powerful), commit a crime, take responsibility, a major breakthrough, pose a threat, strike a balance, play a role, have an impact on, reach a conclusion, draw attention to, raise awareness. Learn the 300 most common IELTS collocations as units — they lift Task 2 and Speaking simultaneously.

    Topic vocabulary banks (learn 10–15 words per theme)

    Environment: carbon emissions, deforestation, renewable energy, biodiversity, sustainable, conservation. Technology: artificial intelligence, automation, digital divide, privacy, innovation. Education: curriculum, literacy, tertiary education, access to schooling, vocational training. Health: obesity, preventive care, sedentary lifestyle, public health. Urban: overpopulation, infrastructure, congestion, urbanisation, public transport. Crime: rehabilitation, deterrent, reoffending, law enforcement. One strong example per theme per essay is plenty.

    Academic verbs for Writing

    Replace weak verbs: get → obtain/receive; make → cause/produce/generate; big → significant/substantial; bad → detrimental/adverse; good → beneficial/advantageous; show → demonstrate/illustrate/reveal; think → argue/contend/maintain. But use them accurately — "demonstrate" needs a finding, "illustrate" needs an example.

    Linking words done right

    Use a range but place them correctly: "However," needs a comma and a contrast; "Therefore" needs a genuine consequence; "Moreover" adds weight (don't overuse — once per essay is enough); "Nevertheless" concedes while holding your line; "Consequently", "In contrast", "On the other hand", "For instance". Mechanical chains ("Firstly, Secondly, Moreover, Furthermore, In conclusion") read as Band 5–6. For Speaking, natural discourse markers beat formal linkers ("Mind you…", "The thing is…").

    How to build it

    Read one quality article daily (BBC News, The Guardian, The Economist) and mine 5 words with their collocations; review in spaced repetition (Anki/Quizlet). Write each new word in an IELTS-style sentence — recognition is not production. Ban memorised lists from your essays; examiners spot unnatural insertions.

    Study Plans, Time Management & Test Day

    Both versions

    The weekly rhythm

    Alternate input days (Reading + Listening) with output days (Writing + Speaking). Every week ends with a full timed mock under exam conditions and a 30-minute error-log review. The error log is the plan's engine: every mistake gets the question type, the reason, and the fix — patterns (always T/F/NG, always articles) become next week's priority.

    4-week sprint (intensive)

    Week 1 — Foundations: full format review (chapters 1–4), timed question-type drills, vocabulary theme 1–2, 1 mock. Week 2 — Writing focus: Task 1 (your version) daily, Task 2 every other day, grammar chapter 10 drills, 1 mock. Week 3 — Speaking & Reading: daily Part 2 practice with recordings, Reading speed drills, vocab themes 3–4, 2 mocks. Week 4 — Exam conditions: full mocks on alternate days, error-log surgery on weak areas, light review only the day before. Daily load: 3–4 hours.

    8-week plan (steady)

    Weeks 1–2: foundations + diagnostic mock; vocabulary themes 1–3. Weeks 3–4: Listening + Reading systems, question-type mastery; 1 mock per week. Week 8: 3 full mocks, error-log review, test-day rehearsal. Daily load: 2 hours.

    Inside the 60 minutes

    Reading: 20/20/20 per passage; never spend 30 on one. Writing: Task 1 — 5 min plan, 12 write, 3 check; Task 2 — 5 plan, 30 write, 5 check. Start with Task 2 (it's worth double). Listening: use every pause to pre-read the next set; check spelling/plurals/word limits during transfer. Speaking: no prep needed in Part 1; use the full minute in Part 2.

    Test-day checklist

    Bring your passport (or the ID you registered with) — no ID, no test. Arrive early; phones stay outside the room. Eat and hydrate normally. In Speaking, greet the examiner warmly and treat it as conversation, not interrogation. If you don't understand, ask ("Could you repeat that, please?") — it costs nothing. Sleep beats last-minute cramming the night before.
