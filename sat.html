    Digital SAT Overview, Format & Scoring

    Reading & Writing / Math

    The Digital SAT is a 2-hour-14-minute, computer-delivered test taken in the College Board's Bluebook app. It has two scored sections — Reading & Writing and Math — each scored 200–800 for a total of 400–1600. Each section is split into two timed modules; after Module 1, the test adapts and routes you to an easier or harder Module 2 based on your performance. There is no penalty for guessing: blank and wrong answers both score zero, so answer every question.

    • Total 2 h 14 min (134 minutes): Reading & Writing 64 min (2 × 32-min modules, 54 questions), Math 70 min (2 × 35-min modules, 44 questions), plus a 10-minute break.
    • Reading & Writing: every passage is short (25–150 words) with exactly one question each — no more long multi-question passages.
    • Math: a built-in Desmos graphing calculator is available on every math question; you may also bring your own approved calculator.
    • Adaptive by module, not by question: your Module 1 accuracy decides whether Module 2 is the harder or easier version. The harder Module 2 is required for top scores.
    • Scoring 400–1600 in 10-point steps (200–800 per section). Subscores and cross-test scores from the paper era are gone.
    • No penalty for wrong answers — never leave a question blank; make your best elimination and guess.
    • Bluebook app required: download it, run the device check, and take a full practice test in the app before test day.

    Reading & Writing: Information and Ideas

    Reading & Writing

    The largest Reading & Writing domain. Information and Ideas questions ask you to find central ideas, make inferences, and use textual or quantitative evidence to support or weaken a claim. Every answer must be directly supported by the passage — the SAT rewards answers you can point to in the text, not answers that merely sound plausible.

    • Central ideas & details: identify the main point; correct answers summarize without adding new claims.
    • Inferences: conclusions must be directly supported — if you cannot point to the line, it is wrong.
    • Command of evidence (textual): pick the quotation that most effectively supports or illustrates the claim.
    • Command of evidence (quantitative): read tables/graphs carefully — match units, labels, and trends to the claim.
    • trap answers: extreme language, half-right/half-wrong, true-but-not-asked.

    Reading & Writing: Craft and Structure

    Reading & Writing

    Craft and Structure tests how authors use language: vocabulary in context, text structure and purpose, and how two texts relate to each other. Words in context questions test meaning-in-context, not memorized definitions — the passage always contains a clue. Text structure questions ask why the author included a sentence; cross-text questions ask how one text responds to another.

    • Words in context: cover the blank, predict a synonym from the clue, then match — never pick the most familiar definition blindly.
    • Text structure & purpose: ask 'what job does this sentence do?' (introduce, contrast, exemplify, conclude).
    • Cross-text connections: read both texts, identify each author's claim, then find the relationship (agree, qualify, refute).
    • Watch for secondary definitions: SAT words often appear in their less-common sense.
    • Purpose questions: correct answers describe function, not content.

    Reading & Writing: Expression of Ideas

    Reading & Writing

    Expression of Ideas is about revision choices: which transition word logically connects two sentences, and which answer choice uses the notes to achieve a stated goal. Transitions questions are won before you read the choices: determine the logical relationship first (contrast, cause, continuation, example, sequence), then pick the word that matches. Rhetorical synthesis questions give bullet notes and a goal — match the goal exactly and verify facts against the notes.

    • Transitions: identify the relationship between the ideas first (contrast, cause-effect, continuation, example, emphasis, sequence), then choose the matching word.
    • Memorize transition categories: contrast (however, nevertheless), cause (therefore, consequently), continuation (moreover, furthermore), example (for instance).
    • Same-category choices can both be eliminated: two correct answers cannot exist, so if however/nevertheless both appear, neither is right.
    • Rhetorical synthesis: read the question's goal first (compare, emphasize similarity, introduce), then pick the choice that achieves exactly that goal.
    • Verify every fact in your chosen synthesis answer against the bullet notes.

    Reading & Writing: Standard English Conventions

    Reading & Writing

    Standard English Conventions is pure grammar: sentence boundaries, punctuation, and verb/modifier rules. The rule set is finite, which makes this the fastest domain to improve. Learn to spot what each question is testing by looking at how the answer choices differ — verbs differing means subject-verb agreement or tense; punctuation differing means boundaries.

    • Sentence boundaries: a period, semicolon, or comma+conjunction can join two independent clauses; a bare comma cannot (comma splice).
    • Colons and dashes: both can introduce an explanation or list after a complete sentence; only one of the two will be offered.
    • Subject-verb agreement: find the true subject (ignore prepositional phrases and parentheticals between subject and verb).
    • Verb tense/form: match the surrounding verbs; watch for -ing fragments that lack a main verb.
    • Modifiers must sit next to what they modify; pronouns need clear, unambiguous antecedents.
    • Apostrophes: its (possessive) vs. it's (it is); plural nouns never take an apostrophe to become plural.

    Reading & Writing: Vocabulary-in-Context & Transitions

    Reading & Writing

    This chapter drills the two highest-frequency micro-skills: precise word choice and logical transitions. Digital SAT vocabulary questions begin each Reading & Writing module and test contextual meaning — every passage plants a synonym or definition clue. Pair the predict-then-match method for words with the relationship-first method for transitions and you will bank the section's easiest points.

    • Predict-then-match: read the sentence with a blank, predict the word's job (positive/negative/neutral), then choose the closest match.
    • Look for the clue: restatements ('in other words'), examples, and contrast pivots ('but', 'however') reveal the intended meaning.
    • Roots, prefixes, suffixes help when you do not know a word — but context outranks etymology.
    • For transitions, determine the relationship (contrast/cause/continuation) before looking at the choices.
    • Cross off same-category transitions: if 'however' and 'nevertheless' both appear, eliminate both.

    Math: Algebra — Linear Equations & Systems

    Math

    Algebra is the SAT Math section's largest domain: linear equations, inequalities, linear functions, and systems of equations. Most systems questions are fastest in Desmos (graph both lines, click the intersection), but you must also handle them by elimination and substitution — and recognize structure questions where the answer follows from the form of the equation without solving.

    • Slope-intercept form : m is the rate of change, b is the starting value; parallel lines share m, perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals.
    • Systems: elimination is fastest when coefficients align; substitution when one variable is isolated; Desmos intersection when numbers are ugly.
    • Infinite solutions: equations describe the same line (one is a multiple of the other). No solution: same slope, different intercept.
    • Word problems: define variables, translate 'is' to =, 'of' to ×, 'per' to ÷; check units at the end.
    • Linear inequalities: flipping the inequality sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative is the classic trap.
    • Structure questions: look at the form — minimum at ; factored form reveals roots directly.

    Desmos Calculator Strategies for Math

    Math

    The built-in Desmos graphing calculator is available on every Math question — the SAT gives it to you on purpose. Students who use Desmos fluently finish faster and make fewer arithmetic errors. The core moves: graph both sides and click intersections, build tables, use sliders for parameters, and run regressions on modeling questions.

    • Graph-and-click: type each side of an equation as its own line; click intersection points to read solutions.
    • Tables: add a table (press +) to evaluate expressions at many x-values instantly.
    • Sliders: add a slider for a constant (e.g., y = ax²) to see how changing a transforms the graph.
    • Regression: for data-modeling questions, type the data as a table and use y1 ~ mx1 + b (linear) or y1 ~ ax1² + bx1 + c (quadratic).
    • Check work: after solving by hand, graph to confirm the answer before moving on.
    • Know the limits: Desmos cannot read the question for you — set up the math first, then let the calculator compute.
    • Practice in the Desmos test mode (desmos.com/practice → College Board) so test day feels familiar.

    Test-Day Strategy, Pacing & Adaptive-Module Tactics

    Strategy

    Score comes from strategy as much as content. Because Module 1 routes your Module 2, treat every Module 1 question as high-stakes — rushing through it to 'warm up' caps your score. Pace at about 1:11 per Reading & Writing question and 1:35 per Math question, mark and skip sticky questions, and use every tool Bluebook gives you (flag, calculator, reference sheet, strikethrough).

    • Module 1 is the routing module: accuracy here decides your Module 2 difficulty — slow down and be careful.
    • Pacing: R&W ≈ 1:11/question (32 min / 27); Math ≈ 1:35/question (35 min / 22). Bank time on easy questions.
    • Two-pass method: answer easy questions first, mark the hard ones, return with remaining time.
    • No guessing penalty — with seconds left, pick one letter for every unanswered question.
    • Use Bluebook tools: flag for review, strikethrough to eliminate choices, the on-screen calculator and formula reference.
    • The night before: light review only, pack ID + admission ticket + approved calculator, sleep 8 hours.
    • Arrive 30 minutes early; eat a real breakfast; bring a snack and water for the break.

    One-Liners — 250 Quick Facts

    Subject-verb agreement

    The verb agrees with the subject, not with nouns inside prepositional phrases: 'The box of chocolates is on the table.'

    Neither/nor agreement

    With 'neither/nor' or 'either/or', the verb agrees with the nearer subject: 'Neither the teacher nor the students were late.'

    Collective nouns

    Team, committee, family take singular verbs when acting as one unit ('The team is winning') and plural when acting individually ('The team are arguing').

    Each / every / either

    'Each', 'every', 'either', 'neither', 'one', 'everyone' are singular: 'Each of the girls has a book.'

    There is / there are

    Match the verb to the true subject after 'there': 'There are many reasons.' Mentally invert: 'Many reasons are there.'

    Dangling modifiers

    An opening phrase must modify the subject: 'Running late, she missed the bus' — 'she' is the one running.

    Misplaced modifiers

    Keep modifiers next to what they describe: 'She only eats vegetables' vs 'She eats only vegetables' mean different things.

    Parallel structure

    Items in a list or comparison must match form: 'She likes hiking, swimming, and biking' — all gerunds.

    Comma splice

    Joining two independent clauses with only a comma is wrong. Fix with a period, semicolon, or comma + conjunction.

    Run-on sentences

    Two independent clauses with no punctuation between them form a run-on. Split or join properly.

    Its vs it's

    'Its' = possessive ('The dog wagged its tail'); 'it's' = 'it is'. The apostrophe never makes a possessive for 'it'.

    Their / there / they're

    'Their' = belonging to them; 'there' = place; 'they're' = 'they are'. Test by substituting the full phrase.

    Who vs whom

    'Who' = subject ('Who called?'); 'whom' = object ('To whom did you speak?'). Substitute 'he'/'him' to decide.

    Which vs that

    'Which' introduces nonrestrictive clauses with commas ('The car, which is red, is mine'); 'that' introduces restrictive clauses without commas.

    Fewer vs less

    'Fewer' for countable nouns ('fewer books'); 'less' for uncountable ('less water').

    Affect vs effect

    'Affect' is usually the verb ('Rain affects crops'); 'effect' is usually the noun ('The effect was huge').

    Then vs than

    'Then' = time/sequence ('First eat, then sleep'); 'than' = comparison ('Taller than me').

    Between vs among

    'Between' for two ('between you and me'); 'among' for three or more ('among the crowd').

    Good vs well

    'Good' is an adjective ('a good book'); 'well' is an adverb ('She sings well').

    Lay vs lie

    'Lay' needs an object ('Lay the book down'); 'lie' does not ('I will lie down'). Past of lie = lay.

    Farther vs further

    'Farther' = physical distance ('farther down the road'); 'further' = figurative ('further discussion').

    Present perfect tense

    'Has/have + past participle' for actions starting in the past continuing now: 'She has lived here since 2015.'

    Past perfect tense

    'Had + past participle' for the earlier of two past events: 'By the time we arrived, the movie had started.'

    Subjunctive mood

    After 'ask/suggest/demand that', use the base verb: 'The teacher demanded that he leave' — not 'leaves'.

    Pronoun case after prepositions

    Objects of prepositions take objective case: 'between you and me', 'for him and her' — never 'I' or 'she'.

    Pronoun-antecedent agreement

    A pronoun must agree in number with its antecedent: 'Every student must bring his or her book' (or rephrase plural).

    Vague pronouns

    'It', 'this', 'they' must point to one clear noun. If the reader asks 'what?', the pronoun is vague.

    Double negatives

    'I don't have no money' is nonstandard — one negative is enough: 'I don't have any money.'

    Apostrophes for possession

    Singular possessive: 'the dog's bone'; plural possessive: 'the dogs' bones'. Apostrophes never form simple plurals.

    Semicolons

    A semicolon joins two closely related independent clauses: 'She ran; he walked.' Both sides must stand alone.

    Colons

    A colon introduces a list, explanation, or example after a complete sentence: 'Bring three things: a pen, paper, and ID.'

    Dashes

    Em dashes set off dramatic interruptions or appositives: 'The winner — exhausted but happy — collapsed.' Use sparingly.

    Quotation punctuation

    In American English, commas and periods go inside quotation marks: She said, 'I agree.'

    Capitalization of titles

    Capitalize the first and last words of titles and all major words: 'The Old Man and the Sea'.

    Comparative vs superlative

    Comparative ('taller') compares two; superlative ('tallest') compares three or more.

    Adjective order

    Standard order: opinion, size, age, color, origin, material — 'a beautiful small old red Italian leather bag'.

    Gerunds vs infinitives

    Some verbs take gerunds ('enjoy swimming'), some infinitives ('want to swim'), some both ('like swimming / to swim').

    Conditional type 1

    Real future possibility: 'If it rains, we will stay home' — if + present, will + base verb.

    Conditional type 2

    Unreal present: 'If I were rich, I would travel' — if + past, would + base verb. Note 'were' for all subjects.

    Conditional type 3

    Unreal past: 'If she had studied, she would have passed' — if + past perfect, would have + participle.

    Active vs passive voice

    Active ('She wrote the essay') is stronger and shorter than passive ('The essay was written by her'). Prefer active.

    Redundancy

    Cut wordy pairs: 'final outcome' → 'outcome'; 'advance planning' → 'planning'; 'the reason is because' → 'the reason is that'.

    Conciseness

    The SAT rewards the shortest grammatically correct choice. If two options say the same thing, pick the shorter.

    Tense consistency

    Do not shift tenses without reason: 'She walked in and sits down' should be 'sat down'.

    Noun number agreement

    'One of the books' (plural noun after 'one of'); 'each student' (singular noun after 'each').

    Data is plural

    'Data' is the plural of 'datum': 'The data are conclusive' — not 'the data is'.

    News is singular

    Despite the -s, 'news' is singular: 'The news was surprising.' Same for 'mathematics', 'physics', 'economics'.

    Number vs amount

    'The number of students has grown' (singular verb — 'number' is the subject); 'A number of students have left' (plural).

    Compared to / compared with

    'Compared to' highlights similarities; 'compared with' highlights differences. Both need the -ed form.

    Different from

    Standard idiom is 'different from' — 'different than' is nonstandard in formal writing.

    Capable of

    Idiom: 'capable of doing' — never 'capable to do'.

    Responsible for

    Idiom: 'responsible for the delay' — not 'responsible of'.

    Between you and me

    Preposition + objective case: 'between you and me' is correct; 'between you and I' is a common hypercorrection.

    Taller than I

    Elliptical comparisons use subject case: 'taller than I (am)' — the verb is simply omitted.

    None of the students

    'None' can be singular or plural; follow the noun: 'None of the cake was eaten' / 'None of the students were late.'

    All of / most of

    'All of the water is gone' (uncountable → singular); 'All of the chairs are taken' (countable → plural).

    Preposition at end

    Ending with a preposition is acceptable in English ('What are you waiting for?'); formality, not grammar, is the issue.

    Split infinitives

    'To boldly go' is grammatically fine — the 'rule' against split infinitives is a myth.

    Who's vs whose

    'Who's' = 'who is'; 'whose' = possessive: 'Whose book is this?'

    Into vs in to

    'Into' = movement toward inside ('walked into the room'); 'in to' = adverb + infinitive ('came in to help').

    Already vs all ready

    'Already' = by now ('already finished'); 'all ready' = fully prepared ('we are all ready').

    Everyday vs every day

    'Everyday' = ordinary (adjective); 'every day' = each day ('I run every day').

    Slope formula

    Slope m = (y2 − y1)/(x2 − x1) — rise over run between two points.

    Slope-intercept form

    y = mx + b: m is slope, b is the y-intercept (where x = 0).

    Point-slope form

    y − y1 = m(x − x1) — fastest way to write a line through a point with known slope.

    Parallel lines

    Parallel lines have equal slopes. Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals (m and −1/m).

    Distance formula

    d = √[(x2−x1)² + (y2−y1)²] — the Pythagorean theorem applied to coordinates.

    Midpoint formula

    Midpoint = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2) — average the x's, average the y's.

    Quadratic standard form

    y = ax² + bx + c. If a > 0 the parabola opens up (minimum); if a < 0 it opens down (maximum).

    Quadratic vertex

    x-coordinate of vertex = −b/(2a). Plug back in for the y-coordinate.

    Vertex form

    y = a(x − h)² + k has vertex at (h, k). Watch the sign: (x − 3)² means h = +3.

    Factoring quadratics

    x² + (a+b)x + ab = (x + a)(x + b). Find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

    Difference of squares

    a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). Spot it instantly: x² − 9 = (x+3)(x−3).

    Perfect square trinomial

    a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)² and a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)².

    Quadratic formula

    x = [−b ± √(b²−4ac)]/(2a). Use when factoring is hard or impossible.

    Discriminant

    b² − 4ac: positive → two real roots; zero → one repeated root; negative → no real roots.

    Sum of roots

    For ax² + bx + c = 0, sum of roots = −b/a and product = c/a (Vieta's formulas).

    Zero product property

    If AB = 0 then A = 0 or B = 0. Always factor first, never divide both sides by x (you lose x = 0).

    Exponent: multiply

    xᵃ · xᵇ = xᵃ⁺ᵇ — add exponents when multiplying same bases.

    Exponent: divide

    xᵃ / xᵇ = xᵃ⁻ᵇ — subtract exponents when dividing same bases.

    Exponent: power of power

    (xᵃ)ᵇ = xᵃᵇ — multiply exponents.

    Negative exponents

    x⁻ᵃ = 1/xᵃ. Move the term across the fraction bar and flip the sign.

    Zero exponent

    x⁰ = 1 for any nonzero x.

    Fractional exponents

    x^(1/n) = ⁿ√x. So x^(2/3) = (∛x)².

    Square root rules

    √(ab) = √a·√b. Simplify by pulling out perfect squares: √50 = 5√2.

    √(x²) = |x|

    The square root is never negative: √(x²) = |x|, not plain x.

    Rationalizing

    Multiply top and bottom by the radical: 1/√2 = √2/2.

    Percent change

    (new − old)/old × 100%. Always divide by the ORIGINAL value.

    Percent of

    'p% of N' = (p/100) × N. Convert the percent to a decimal first.

    Successive percent changes

    Multiply the multipliers: +10% then −10% = 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99 → 1% net decrease.

    Simple interest

    I = Prt — principal × rate × time. Interest only, not total.

    Compound growth

    A = P(1 + r)ᵗ. Each period multiplies by (1 + r).

    Average (mean)

    Sum of values ÷ number of values. To find a missing value, use: sum = mean × count.

    Median

    The middle value of an ordered list. For an even count, average the two middle values.

    Mode

    The most frequent value. A data set can have no mode or multiple modes.

    Range

    Maximum − minimum. Quick but sensitive to outliers.

    Standard deviation

    Measures spread around the mean. Larger SD = more spread out. (60−50)/5 = 2 SDs above mean.

    Weighted average

    Multiply each value by its weight, sum, divide by total weight — like GPA calculation.

    Ratios

    a:b with total T: a's share = a/(a+b) × T. Add the parts first.

    Direct variation

    y = kx. Find k from one pair, then apply to the other.

    Inverse variation

    y = k/x, so xy = k (constant). More workers → fewer days: total worker-days stays fixed.

    Probability basics

    P = favorable outcomes / total outcomes. Probabilities always lie between 0 and 1.

    Independent events

    P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). 'With replacement' keeps probabilities constant.

    Without replacement

    Denominators shrink: P(both green) = (5/8) × (4/7). Adjust after each draw.

    Expected counts

    Survey percent × population = expected number: 35% of 200 = 70.

    Margin of error

    A 60% ± 4% result means the plausible range is 56%–64% — it extends both ways.

    Correlation ≠ causation

    Strong correlation proves association only. A third factor may drive both variables.

    Line of best fit

    Use its equation to predict: y = 2.5x + 10 → at x = 6, y = 25.

    Positive vs negative correlation

    Points rising left-to-right = positive; falling = negative; cloud = weak/zero.

    Circle: area & circumference

    A = πr²; C = 2πr = πd. Given area 25π, r = 5.

    Arc length

    (θ/360°) × 2πr. A 60° arc in radius-6 circle = 2π.

    Sector area

    (θ/360°) × πr². A 90° sector of radius 4 = 4π.

    Pythagorean theorem

    a² + b² = c². Memorize triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 6-8-10, 9-12-15.

    45-45-90 triangle

    Legs equal; hypotenuse = leg√2. Hypotenuse 10 → legs 5√2.

    30-60-90 triangle

    Sides in ratio 1 : √3 : 2 (short leg : long leg : hypotenuse). Short leg 7 → hypotenuse 14.

    SOH CAH TOA

    sin = opposite/hypotenuse; cos = adjacent/hypotenuse; tan = opposite/adjacent.

    Pythagorean identity

    sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, always.

    Triangle angle sum

    Interior angles sum to 180°. Exterior angle = sum of the two remote interiors.

    Polygon interior angles

    (n − 2) × 180°. Hexagon = 720°.

    Similar triangles

    Corresponding sides are proportional. Scale factor = new/old; apply to every side.

    Area of triangle

    ½ × base × height. For right triangles, the legs are base and height.

    Trapezoid area

    ½ × (base1 + base2) × height.

    Rectangle: perimeter & area

    P = 2(l + w); A = lw.

    Volume: prism

    V = length × width × height.

    Volume: cylinder

    V = πr²h.

    Volume: cone

    V = (1/3)πr²h — one-third of the cylinder.

    Command of Evidence: the core skill

    Most 'Information and Ideas' questions ask what the text supports. The answer is always directly backed by specific lines — find them before choosing.

    Main idea in 25–150 words

    Digital SAT passages are tiny. The main idea is usually stated in the first or last sentence. Do not overthink it.

    Inference boundaries

    A valid inference stays inside the passage. If an answer adds new facts or goes one step too far, eliminate it.

    Quantitative evidence questions

    When a passage cites a graph or table, the answer must match BOTH the text claim and the data. Check numbers, not vibes.

    Strengthen vs weaken

    'Strengthen' = find the choice that supports the hypothesis; 'weaken' = find the one that undermines it. One word flips the task — read carefully.

    The 'too strong' trap

    Choices with always, never, only, or completely usually overstate the passage. The SAT prefers qualified language: often, may, can.

    The 'true but irrelevant' trap

    A choice can be factually true and still wrong if it does not answer the question asked. Match the question stem exactly.

    Reading the question first

    With one question per short passage, read the question stem first so your single read-through is targeted.

    Paired evidence sets

    Some questions ask for the quote that best supports the previous answer. Treat them as a pair: lock the claim, then match the lines.

    Data + text synthesis

    Read the text's claim first, then check the data. The right choice is the one where text and numbers agree.

    Author's main purpose

    Ask: what does the author WANT? To explain, argue, describe, or question? Purpose ≠ topic.

    Dealing with unfamiliar topics

    30 September 2026

    You never need background knowledge. Everything needed is in the passage — outside facts can actually mislead you.

    Time per Reading question

    About 70 seconds per question in each 32-minute module. If one takes 2+ minutes, flag it and move on.

    Annotating in Bluebook

    Highlight the sentence containing the claim or data as you read. On evidence questions you will jump straight back to it.

    Words in Context method

    1) Cover the choices. 2) Predict a word from the surrounding clues. 3) Pick the choice matching your prediction. Clues are always nearby.

    Secondary meanings

    The SAT loves familiar words used unusually: 'flag' (to weaken), 'check' (to restrain), 'qualify' (to limit). Read the sentence, not your habits.

    Positive/negative charge

    If you cannot pin the exact word, at least get its charge. 'The committee ___ the proposal' with positive context eliminates negative choices.

    Text structure patterns

    Common structures: problem→solution, claim→evidence, theory→counterexample, phenomenon→explanation. Name the pattern to nail purpose questions.

    Purpose of a detail

    Ask 'why did the author include this?' — details illustrate, support, qualify, or contrast. They are never random.

    Cross-text questions

    Summarize each text's claim in 5 words first. Then the relationship (agree/disagree/qualify) becomes obvious.

    Author's tone

    Tone comes from word choice: 'claims' vs 'argues' vs 'demonstrates' signal different attitudes. Match the charge of the passage.

    Rhetorical questions

    A question in the passage is never really asking you — it is provoking thought or framing doubt. Its function is persuasive, not interrogative.

    Analogies

    Analogies make the unfamiliar familiar. On purpose questions, the answer is about CLARIFYING, not about the analogy's literal subject.

    Concession moves

    'While X, Y' structures concede a point to strengthen the main claim. The author's real position is the Y clause.

    Ironic endings

    When a passage ends by flipping its opening image, the function is irony or paradox — a favorite SAT structure move.

    Narrative perspective

    First-person narrators can be unreliable; third-person omniscient narrators are not. Perspective shapes what the text can claim.

    Vocabulary in context vs definitions

    The SAT never asks for dictionary definitions — it asks what the word means IN THIS SENTENCE. Context always wins.

    Eliminating half-right choices

    30 September 2026

    A choice that is half-right is fully wrong. If any part contradicts the passage, eliminate the whole choice.

    Transitions: the 4 families

    Contrast (however, although), Cause-effect (therefore, consequently), Continuation (moreover, furthermore), Example (for instance). Classify first, then choose.

    The same-category rule

    If two choices belong to the same transition family, both are wrong — the test cannot have two correct answers. Eliminate both.

    However vs therefore

    'However' reverses direction; 'therefore' continues it. Determine whether sentence 2 continues or reverses sentence 1 — that single decision answers most transition questions.

    Subtle contrast markers

    'Surprisingly', 'nevertheless', 'despite this', 'in contrast' all mark contrast. 'Similarly', 'likewise', 'in addition' mark continuation.

    Rhetorical synthesis: goal first

    Read the student's GOAL before the notes. The goal ('emphasize the scale', 'explain why') is the grading rubric.

    Rhetorical synthesis: verify facts

    Every fact in your chosen answer must appear in the notes. A choice that adds outside info is wrong, however elegant.

    Combining sentences

    The best combination is the most concise one that preserves all meaning and stays grammatical. Shorter = better, all else equal.

    Placement of details

    Details belong where they are most relevant. A sentence about methods goes with methods, not with conclusions.

    Conciseness wins

    On 'which choice is most effective' questions, wordy options that add nothing are always wrong. Cut ruthlessly.

    Maintaining focus

    A sentence that introduces a new, unrelated topic breaks focus. The right revision keeps the paragraph on its established subject.

    Logical order

    Ideas should flow: general→specific, cause→effect, problem→solution. A sentence that jumps ahead belongs later.

    Emphasis techniques

    What comes last gets emphasis; what is repeated gets emphasis. Authors place key claims in these power positions.

    Precision in word choice

    'Huge' vs 'significant' vs 'unprecedented' carry different strengths. Match the word's strength to the evidence's strength.

    Avoiding redundancy

    'The final outcome was successful' says 'successful' twice. The SAT punishes redundancy — one clear word beats three vague ones.

    The subject-verb shortcut

    Cross out prepositional phrases first: 'The list of names IS long.' The true subject is revealed once clutter is removed.

    Tricky subjects

    Each, every, either, neither, one, everyone = singular. 'Each of the players IS ready' — never let the plural noun fool you.

    Neither/nor proximity rule

    The verb matches the NEARER subject: 'Neither the coach nor the players WERE happy.' Check what is closest to the verb.

    Tense logic

    'Since 2015' → present perfect (has lived). Two past events → past perfect for the earlier one (had left). Time markers dictate tense.

    Subjunctive triggers

    Demand, suggest, require, ask + 'that' → base verb: 'She suggested that he GO.' No -s ending, ever.

    Dangling modifier test

    30 September 2026

    After an opening phrase, the subject must be the doer: 'Walking home, ___' needs a person, not 'the rain'.

    Pronoun case cheat

    Prepositions need objective case: between you and ME, for HIM and HER. Cover the other person and test: 'for I' sounds wrong because it is.

    Its vs it's — final word

    If you can replace it with 'it is', use it's. Otherwise its. 'The company raised its prices' — never 'it's prices'.

    Comma splice repair kit

    Two independent clauses? Join with (a) period, (b) semicolon, or (c) comma + and/but/so. Never a bare comma.

    Semicolon rule

    Both sides of a semicolon must be complete sentences: 'She ran; he walked.' If one side is a fragment, the semicolon is wrong.

    Colon rule

    A colon needs a complete sentence BEFORE it: 'She packed three things: shoes, books, snacks.' Never after 'are' or 'include'.

    Which needs commas

    'Which' clauses are nonrestrictive and need commas: 'The house, which is old, sold fast.' 'That' clauses take no commas.

    Apostrophe discipline

    Apostrophes show possession or contraction — never plurals. 'Tomatoes', not 'tomato's'.

    Parallelism check

    Lists and comparisons must match form: 'She enjoys hiking, swimming, and biking.' Mixed forms are always wrong.

    Idiom bank

    Capable OF, responsible FOR, different FROM, based ON, in addition TO. Idioms are memorization — keep a personal list.

    Conciseness as tiebreaker

    When two choices are both grammatical, the shorter one that preserves meaning is correct. Wordiness is the SAT's favorite wrong answer.

    Solving linear equations

    Isolate x step by step: undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division. Check by plugging back in.

    Slope from two points

    (y2−y1)/(x2−x1). Through (2,3) and (6,11): 8/4 = 2. Rise over run — always.

    Reading y = mx + b

    m = steepness and direction; b = where the line crosses the y-axis. Negative m slopes downhill.

    Elimination for systems

    Line up the equations and add/subtract to kill one variable. x+y=10, x−y=4 → add → 2x=14.

    Substitution for systems

    When one variable is already isolated (y = 2x+1), substitute it into the other equation. Fast and clean.

    Function evaluation

    f(a) means replace every x with a. f(x)=2x²−5 → f(3)=2(9)−5=13. Watch negative inputs carefully.

    Absolute value equations

    |x−3|=7 splits into two equations: x−3=7 and x−3=−7. Solutions: 10 and −4. Never forget the negative case.

    Inequalities

    Same as equations, except: flip the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

    Word problems: define first

    Let x = what you are asked for. Write the equation in words first ('cost = 20 + 0.10 × minutes'), then translate.

    Percent word problems

    'What percent of 80 is 20?' → 20/80 = 25%. Always divide by the ORIGINAL (the 'of' number).

    Ratio word problems

    Boys:girls = 3:4 with 28 total → 7 parts, 4 per part → 16 girls. Add parts, divide total, multiply.

    Rate problems

    Distance = rate × time. 240 miles in 4 hours = 60 mph. Keep units consistent.

    Consecutive integers

    Let them be n, n+1, n+2. Sum problems become one equation: n + (n+1) + (n+2) = total.

    Age problems

    Make a now/later table. 'In 5 years' means add 5 to BOTH people's ages.

    Mixture problems

    30 September 2026

    Track the pure substance: amount × concentration, before and after. The total of the pure stuff is conserved.

    Work problems

    Combined rate = sum of individual rates. A does 1/6 of the job per hour, B does 1/3 → together 1/2 per hour → 2 hours.

    Factoring quadratics fast

    x²+7x+10: find two numbers multiplying to 10, adding to 7 → 2 and 5 → (x+2)(x+5).

    Zero product property

    If (x−2)(x−3)=0, then x=2 or x=3. Factor first — never divide both sides by x (you lose the x=0 solution).

    Vertex shortcut

    x = −b/(2a) gives the vertex's x-coordinate instantly. For x²−4x+3: x=2, then y=−1.

    Discriminant decoder

    b²−4ac > 0: two real roots. = 0: one repeated root. < 0: no real roots (parabola misses the x-axis).

    Vieta's formulas

    For ax²+bx+c=0: sum of roots = −b/a, product = c/a. Answer 'sum of roots' questions in seconds.

    Completing the square

    x²+8x → add (8/2)²=16 → (x+4)²−16. The number added is always (b/2)².

    Exponent rules master

    Multiply: add. Divide: subtract. Power of power: multiply. Negative: flip. Zero: equals 1.

    Radical simplification

    Pull out perfect squares: √50=√(25×2)=5√2. Look for the largest square factor.

    Rational expressions

    Factor everything first, then cancel common factors: (x²−4)/(x−2) = x+2 (x≠2).

    Remainder theorem

    Dividing a polynomial by (x−a) leaves remainder = P(a). Plug in a — no long division needed.

    Polynomial identities

    (a+b)²=a²+2ab+b². The middle term 2ab is the classic trap — (2x−3)²=4x²−12x+9, not 4x²−9.

    Nonlinear systems

    Line meets parabola in 0, 1, or 2 points. Substitute the line into the quadratic and check the discriminant.

    Equivalent expressions

    'Equivalent' means identical for all x. Expand, factor, or test with a value like x=2 to eliminate choices.

    Isolating radicals

    Get the radical alone, then square both sides: √(x+3)=5 → x+3=25 → x=22. Check for extraneous roots.

    Quadratic word problems

    Max/min questions are vertex questions in disguise. Model with a quadratic, find −b/(2a).

    Negative leading coefficient

    y=−x²+6x−5 opens DOWNWARD — the vertex is a maximum. Sign of 'a' decides up vs down.

    Percent change formula

    (New−Old)/Old × 100%. 50→65 is 30%, not 23%. The denominator is always the starting value.

    Percent of a number

    Convert first: 35% of 200 = 0.35×200 = 70. For 0.25% of 800: 0.0025×800 = 2.

    Reverse percentages

    $40 after 20% off → 40 = 0.8×original → original = $50. Divide by the multiplier, don't add 20%.

    Simple vs compound

    Simple: I=Prt (linear). Compound: multiply by (1+r) each period. $1000 at 5% for 2 years → $1102.50.

    Mean vs median

    Mean uses every value (sensitive to outliers); median is the middle (resistant). Outlier 18 in {2,4,4,4,6,18} pulls the mean above the median.

    Reading scatterplots

    Rising dots = positive correlation; falling = negative; shapeless cloud = weak. Correlation never proves causation.

    Line of best fit

    It summarizes the trend. Plug the given x into its equation to predict y — that is the whole task.

    Survey validity

    Results generalize only if the sample represents the population. Surveying gym members about exercise habits is biased.

    Margin of error

    60%±4% means the true value plausibly lies between 56% and 64%. It cuts both ways.

    Probability without replacement

    Each draw changes the denominator: (5/8)×(4/7) for two greens from 5G+3Y. 'Without replacement' is the signal.

    Two-dice probabilities

    36 total outcomes. Sum of 7 has 6 ways → 6/36 = 1/6. List the pairs when unsure.

    Unit conversion traps

    Check that the question's units match the answer choices' units. Minutes vs hours is the classic trap.

    Estimation first

    Round to friendly numbers (19.8×5.1 ≈ 20×5 = 100) to eliminate absurd choices before computing.

    Ratios in tables

    Read the table's labels twice. 'Percent of surveyed' vs 'percent of population' are different claims.

    Growth rate math

    $2M→$3M over 3 years is NOT 50%/year. Annual rate = (3/2)^(1/3)−1 ≈ 14.5%.

    Categorical data

    Two-way tables: 'given that' means restrict to the row/column first, then compute the fraction.

    Pythagorean triples

    Memorize 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 6-8-10, 9-12-15. Spotting them saves a square root every time.

    Special right triangles

    45-45-90: legs equal, hyp = leg√2. 30-60-90: sides 1:√3:2. These two patterns cover most trig questions.

    SOH CAH TOA

    Sin=opp/hyp, Cos=adj/hyp, Tan=opp/adj. sin(A)=3/5 → it's a 3-4-5 → cos(A)=4/5.

    Circle formulas

    Area πr², circumference 2πr. Sector/arc: multiply by θ/360. Radius 4, 90° sector → 4π.

    Parallel lines & transversals

    Corresponding angles equal, alternate interior equal, consecutive supplementary. One angle unlocks them all.

    Triangle angle facts

    Sum 180°. Isosceles: base angles equal. Equilateral: all 60°. Exterior = sum of remote interiors.

    Volume formulas

    Prism: lwh. Cylinder: πr²h. Cone: ⅓πr²h. Sphere: ⁴⁄₃πr³. The SAT reference sheet gives these — know which to use.

    Coordinate geometry

    Distance = √[(Δx)²+(Δy)²]. Midpoint = averages. Slope of perpendicular = negative reciprocal.

    Transformations

    Reflect over x-axis: (x,y)→(x,−y). Translate right 5, down 2: (x+5, y−2). Track each coordinate separately.

    Circle equation

    (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r². Center (2,−3), radius 5 → (x−2)²+(y+3)²=25. Signs flip inside.

    Similar figures

    Corresponding lengths scale by k; AREAS scale by k². Hypotenuse 5→15 (k=3) means shortest side 3→9.

    Complementary & supplementary

    Complementary = 90°. Supplementary = 180°. Differ-by-20 complementaries: (90+20)/2 = 55° for the larger.

    Polygons

    Interior sum = (n−2)×180°. Hexagon = 720°. Regular hexagon interior angle = 120°.

    The unit circle basics

    cos(60°)=1/2, sin(30°)=1/2. These come straight from the 30-60-90 triangle.

    Area shortcuts

    Square from diagonal: A=d²/2. Right triangle: legs are base and height. Trapezoid: ½(b1+b2)h.

    Geometry word problems

    30 September 2026

    Draw the figure and label everything. Most geometry errors come from skipping the sketch.
