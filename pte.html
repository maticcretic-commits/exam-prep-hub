    Overview

    PTE Academic is a 2-hour, fully computer-based English test scored 10–90 by automated AI scoring. Three parts run back to back: Speaking & Writing, then Reading, then Listening. Many tasks are integrated — Read Aloud feeds Speaking + Reading, Repeat Sentence feeds Speaking + Listening, Write from Dictation feeds Listening + Writing — so smart preparation targets high-leverage tasks first.

    What changed recently: the August 2025 update added two new speaking tasks (Respond to a Situation, Summarize Group Discussion), extended the test to about 2 hours 15 minutes, and introduced human review alongside AI scoring for 7 tasks (Describe Image, Retell Lecture, Summarize Written Text, Write Essay, Summarize Spoken Text and the two new tasks). This hub reflects the current format.

    Task tactics (50)

    TacticRead Aloud: 30–40s silent prep — mark hard words, phrase breaks, numbers — then read 50–60 words in 40s.
    TacticRead Aloud feeds TWO scores: Speaking + Reading — word accuracy matters as much as fluency.
    TacticNever correct a misread word aloud — backtracking kills Oral Fluency more than the slip cost.
    Tactic3 seconds of silence ends any speaking recording — keep sound flowing, never freeze.
    TacticRepeat Sentence: 10–12 items, audio once (3–9s), 15s to repeat — chunk into 3–4 meaning phrases.
    TacticRepeat Sentence scoring: Content 3 (all) / 2 (>50%) / 1 (<50%) / 0 + Fluency + Pronunciation = 8 pts.
    TacticWord endings decide Repeat Sentence: plurals, -ed, articles — train your ear on weak forms.
    TacticDescribe Image: 25s prep, 40s talk — intro → 2–3 key features with numbers → trend conclusion.
    TacticDescribe Image + Retell Lecture are double-scored (AI + human) since late 2024 — real content beats waffle.
    TacticRetell Lecture: lecture up to 90s, 10s prep, 40s retell — notes = keywords + numbers + arrows.
    TacticAnswer Short Question: 10s, one or two words, vocabulary-only scoring — guess rather than stay silent.
    TacticSummarize Written Text: ONE sentence, 5–75 words, 10 min — two sentences = Form zero.
    TacticSWT method: thesis + 2–3 key points fused with although / because / which / whereas.
    TacticWrite Essay: ONE essay, 20 minutes, 200–300 words — target 240.
    TacticEssay traits (7): Content, Form, Development/Structure/Coherence, Grammar, Range, Vocabulary, Spelling.
    TacticEssay plan: 3–4 min plan, 13–14 min write, 2–3 min check — always verify word count.
    TacticR&W Fill in the Blanks: 1 pt per blank, no negative marking — never leave a gap empty.
    TacticFIB strategy: read the whole text first, then fill by grammar slot + collocation.
    TacticTop FIB grammar: articles, prepositions, verb forms, word-family suffixes (-tion, -ive, -ly).
    TacticCollocations win FIB: ‘come into effect’, ‘play a vital role’, ‘draw a conclusion’ — learn 200.
    TacticRe-order Paragraphs: find the topic sentence (no backward pronouns), then chain ‘they/this/however’.
    TacticRe-order scoring: 1 pt per correct adjacent pair — partial orders still bank marks.
    TacticMCQ Multiple Answers: +1 correct / −1 wrong, floor 0 — only tick text-supported options.
    TacticReading ≈ 29–30 min: ~2 min per FIB text, brisk on single MCQs, watch the clock.
    TacticSST: 50–70 words, 10 min, 3–4 sentences — feeds Writing + Listening.
    TacticSST notes: 8–12 keywords during audio — never write full sentences while listening.
    TacticHighlight Correct Summary: pre-read the 4 options, note what differs, listen for the decider.
    TacticSelect Missing Word: follow intonation + logic — the sentence’s momentum reveals the ending.
    TacticListening FIB: type the word the instant you hear it — spelling must be exact.
    TacticWFD: hear once, chunk into phrases, then type — every word incl. ‘a/the/-s’ is a point.
    TacticWFD: 3–4 sentences per test, Listening + Writing — the most bankable task with practice.
    TacticHIW: +1 per true mismatch, −1 per wrong click, floor 0 — click only sure mismatches.
    TacticHIW technique: cursor tracks just ahead of the audio — mismatches pop out.
    TacticOverall scale 10–90: overall + Speaking, Writing, Reading, Listening — all AI-scored.
    TacticPart order: Speaking & Writing → Reading → Listening; total about 2 hours.
    TacticMic position: a fist’s width, slightly below mouth, never moved mid-section.
    TacticTest-centre noise is normal — steady volume beats loud bursts; never touch the mic.
    TacticSpelling: pick British OR American and stay consistent — mixing costs Spelling trait marks.
    TacticHomophone traps in WFD: affect/effect, their/there, its/it’s — let the grammar slot decide.
    TacticNever submit blanks: FIB, WFD, single MCQs have no negative marking — guesses gain.
    TacticShadowing 10 min/day (repeat 1s behind a native speaker) rewires Repeat Sentence skill.
    TacticParaphrase in SWT/SST/Retell — heavy copying caps Content and Vocabulary traits.
    TacticEmergency Reading protocol (3 min left): guess every FIB blank + quick MCQs, skip nothing.
    TacticFront-load SST summaries with the lecturer’s main message — core content banked first.
    TacticDescribe Image dry at 25s? Compare highest vs lowest, restate trend, note an exception.
    TacticEssay development = Point → explain mechanism → example → link to question.
    TacticConclusion: restate position + 2 reasons in 2–3 sentences — never add new arguments.
    TacticConsistent spelling variety + proofread articles/verb forms in the last 2 minutes of essays.
    TacticDescribe Image maps: describe concentrations, routes and patterns — data, not decoration.
    TacticASQ flashcards: capitals, organs, instruments, occupations — one-word instant recall, timed.

    1 Oct 2026 — A new Class 9 textbook traces institution mottos, including 'Satyamev Jayate' (Mundaka Upanishad), to ancient Indian texts.

    Format & Scoring

    The 2-hour sprint

    PTE Academic runs about 2 hours, computer-based, in one sitting: Part 1 Speaking & Writing → Part 2 Reading → Part 3 Listening. No breaks between parts — stamina is part of the test. Eat beforehand; the clock never pauses.

    Format & Scoring

    The 10–90 scale

    Every score — overall plus Speaking, Writing, Reading, Listening — runs 10 to 90. Universities typically ask 58–65+ overall; competitive programs want 65–79. Know your target band before you book: preparation differs sharply between ‘pass 50’ and ‘each 79’.

    Format & Scoring

    AI scoring, explained

    Your speaking and writing are scored by automated engines trained on thousands of human-rated responses. They measure acoustic and textual features — fluency smoothness, pronunciation clarity, grammar patterns — not ‘understanding’. Since late 2024, Describe Image and Retell Lecture get human review too.

    Format & Scoring

    Integrated scoring is the cheat code

    Many tasks feed two skills: Read Aloud → Speaking + Reading; Repeat Sentence → Speaking + Listening; WFD → Listening + Writing. A single strong task lifts two columns of your score report — prioritise these in preparation.

    Format & Scoring 30 September 2026

    Partial credit is everywhere

    Almost nothing is all-or-nothing: FIB blanks pay per blank, Re-order pays per pair, WFD pays per word. The test rewards attempting everything — blanks are the only guaranteed zero. Guess aggressively where there is no negative marking.

    Format & Scoring

    Negative marking: the two traps

    Only MCQ Multiple Answers (Reading and Listening) and Highlight Incorrect Words punish wrong picks (−1, floored at 0). Everywhere else, wrong answers cost nothing. Learn which items are which — it changes your guessing strategy completely.

    Format & Scoring

    Enabling skills are gone

    Older score reports showed Grammar, Fluency, Pronunciation as separate ‘enabling skills’. They still exist inside scoring — as traits within tasks — but your report now shows just overall + four communicative skills. Focus on task traits, not the old labels.

    Format & Scoring

    What ‘communicative skills’ really means

    Your four scores measure what you can do with English: speak, write, read, listen. Each is built from multiple tasks, so one weak task never sinks a skill alone — but one strong integrated task can rescue it. Breadth across tasks beats perfection in one.

    Format & Scoring 30 September 2026

    The 2025 update in 30 seconds

    Recent changes (August 2025): two new speaking tasks (Respond to a Situation, Summarize Group Discussion), test extended to about 2 hours 15 minutes, and human review alongside AI scoring for 7 tasks. If your materials predate 2025, verify they reflect this.

    Format & Scoring

    Score validity and resits

    PTE scores are typically valid 2 years for admissions and visas (check your institution). You can retake as often as you like, but must wait for results (usually within 48 hours, often much faster) before booking again. There is no limit on attempts.

    Read Aloud

    RA: the 35-second ritual

    Prep (30–40s): read silently, mark hard words, note numbers, find phrase breaks. Record (40s): newsreader pace, phrase naturally, never correct yourself. Done? Click Next — don’t wait out the clock.

    Read Aloud

    Why RA is the highest-ROI task

    6–7 items × two skill scores (Speaking + Reading) = the densest points-per-minute in the test. A candidate who lifts RA from average to strong can gain 5–10 points on two skills at once. Drill it daily; nothing else pays like it.

    Read Aloud

    The 3-second rule

    Silence longer than 3 seconds closes the microphone on every speaking task. Hesitation is the only fatal error — worse than any mispronunciation. If you lose your place: keep vocalising, find your place while talking, never freeze.

    Read Aloud

    Pronunciation ≠ accent

    The engine is trained on global Englishes — Indian, Chinese, Latin American accents all score fully when speech is clear. What loses marks: swallowed syllables, dropped endings, mumbling. Clarity, not accent reduction, is the goal.

    Read Aloud

    Numbers, symbols, abbreviations

    Decide renderings in prep time: 1998 → ‘nineteen ninety-eight’, 45% → ‘forty-five percent’, e.g. → ‘for example’, CO₂ → ‘C-O-two’. Stalling on a symbol mid-recording breaks fluency — pre-decide every token.

    Read Aloud

    RA content: word-by-word accounting

    Content is mechanical: each text word is marked correct / substituted / omitted. Inserted extra words are ignored for content. So: attempt every word, skip nothing deliberately, and don’t improvise — improvisation can’t earn, but skipping loses.

    Read Aloud

    Breathing without breaking fluency

    Pause silently at phrase boundaries (commas, clause ends) — natural and unscored. Never fill pauses with ‘um’/‘uh’; fillers register as hesitation. Mark 2–3 breath points during prep so pauses land where they belong.

    Read Aloud

    The mic check matters

    The unscored equipment check at the start is your only calibration: set the mic a fist’s width below your mouth, speak normally, confirm the level. Then don’t touch it. Most ‘the mic didn’t hear me’ disasters trace to a skipped or rushed check.

    Read Aloud

    RA practice loop

    Daily: pick a 55-word news paragraph → 35s silent prep → record 40s → listen back, count misreads and hesitations. Two weeks of this loop visibly moves RA scores — it trains the exact test motion, not generic ‘speaking’.

    Read Aloud

    When the text is hard

    Dense academic text? Prioritise getting through it smoothly over perfect decoding. Attempt each hard word once, keep rhythm, keep going. A smooth pass with 3 misreads outscores a hesitant pass with 1 — fluency weight is real.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    RS: the memory game

    10–12 sentences, heard once, 3–9 seconds each. Listen for meaning chunks (who-did-what), jot content-word initials, then rebuild smoothly in 15s. Perfect recall is rare — fluent 70% beats hesitant 95% on total points.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    Chunking: how memory really works

    Short-term memory holds ~4 chunks, not 12 words. Hear ‘The new policy / will affect / international students / from next semester’ as four ideas and you can rebuild it; hear twelve loose words and they evaporate. Train chunking, not cramming.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    Weak forms: the invisible words

    Native speech blurs auxiliaries, articles, prepositions (‘might’ve’, ‘of’, ‘to’) — exactly the words RS scores. Shadow native audio 10 min/day: repeat 1 second behind the speaker, copying rhythm. Your ear learns to catch what your mouth must reproduce.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    Describe Image: the universal template

    1. ‘This [chart/map/diagram] shows X.’ 2. Key features with 2–3 numbers. 3. Highest/lowest comparison. 4. Trend + conclusion. 40 seconds, no gaps. The template works on bar, line, pie, table, map, process — learn it once, use it everywhere.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    DI content: relationships, not inventory

    Content rewards trends, comparisons, extremes — ‘rose sharply’, ‘twice as high as’, ‘peaked in 2020’. Reciting every data point is not content; it’s reading. Two sharp insights beat ten flat numbers, especially under human review.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    The process-diagram walkthrough

    For cycles and processes: name it (‘This diagram illustrates the stages of…’), then walk first → next → finally with linking words. Each stage = one sentence. Passive voice fits naturally (‘the water is heated’) — use it.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    DI when you go blank at 20 seconds

    Stretch toolkit: compare two values, restate the trend differently, note an exception, project an implication. Never announce you’re done. Twenty more seconds of relevant speech always exists — mine it.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    RS + DI: the listening-speaking bridge

    Both feed Speaking + Listening. Together with Read Aloud, Part 1’s big three decide your Listening score before the Listening section even starts. Weak at listening MCQs? These integrated tasks are your compensation strategy.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    Intonation is free points

    Mimic the RS speaker’s melody; use newsreader intonation in DI. Natural stress and pitch movement feed the Fluency trait directly. Monotone delivery — even if accurate — leaves fluency marks unclaimed.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image 30 September 2026

    The ‘don’t fix it’ rule

    Applies to every speaking task: never stop to correct yourself. The fluency engine penalises restarts and self-repair far more than content penalises one wrong word. Forward motion always. Always.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    RL: the 90-second challenge

    Lecture up to 90s → 10s prep → 40s retell. Notes during audio: topic, 2–3 main points, numbers, stance words (‘argued’, ‘compared’). Then deliver a mini-talk: topic sentence, points with linkers, wrap-up. Notes are everything — no notes, no content.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    Paraphrase, don’t parrot

    Content scores ideas, not quotation. Rebuilding the lecture’s points in your own words scores fully and is far more reliable than half-remembered verbatim chunks — which derail fluency when they collapse mid-sentence. Meaning over wording.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    The skeleton-first principle

    If notes are thin, deliver the skeleton: topic + whatever main points survived + the speaker’s stance. A smooth partial retell earns content-partial + full fluency/pronunciation. Silence earns zero everywhere. Structure what you have; don’t mourn what’s missing.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ: the cheapest points in the test

    5–6 items, 10 seconds each, scored on vocabulary alone — fluency and pronunciation don’t count. One right word = 1 point. There is no reason to lose these: answer instantly, guess when unsure, never leave one blank.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ drill: the flashcard sprint

    Make 200 cards: question on front, one-word answer on back — capitals, organs, instruments, occupations, common facts. Drill against a 10-second timer. ASQ tests retrieval speed, not knowledge depth; train the reflex.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ trap: the near-synonym

    ‘Astronomer’ vs ‘astrologer’, ‘invent’ vs ‘discover’ — ASQ punishes the almost-right word. Learn the canonical term for each concept. When two words compete in your head, pick the textbook one.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    RL + DI: one skill, two inputs

    Retell Lecture is Describe Image with audio: same 40-second structured talk, same no-silence rule, same linkers. Master the DI delivery frame and RL becomes a note-taking problem only — half the task is already trained.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    Stance words unlock RL content

    Lecturers signal structure: ‘the main reason’, ‘however’, ‘for example’, ‘in contrast’. These markers flag what matters — note the point that follows each one. Listening for markers beats transcribing sentences.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ: say it, don’t frame it

    ‘Ice’ beats ‘The frozen form of water is called ice.’ Every extra word is wasted time and zero extra credit. One or two words, immediately. Brevity is the format’s whole strategy.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    The 10-second ASQ clock

    Ten seconds feels long until you’re unsure — then it vanishes. Decide your guess threshold: 4 seconds. No answer by 4s? Say your best related word. A fast wrong guess and a slow right answer score the same as each other’s opposites — speed matters.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT: one sentence to rule them all

    Exactly one sentence. 5–75 words. 10 minutes. Fail either rule → Form zero. The compression skill: thesis + 2–3 key points fused with subordinators (although, because, which, whereas). Draft to ~60 words, then count.

    Summarize Written Text

    The SWT fuse box

    Subordinate clauses are your compression tools: ‘Although X, Y because Z, which led to W.’ Three ideas, one sentence, fully grammatical. Practise fusing any two news sentences with ‘although’ until it’s automatic.

    Summarize Written Text

    Copying vs borrowing

    Borrow terms, rebuild grammar. Lifting whole clauses from the passage caps Content and Vocabulary — the scorer wants evidence you processed the text. Keep the key nouns; rewrite everything around them in your own structure.

    Summarize Written Text

    The second-full-stop killer

    Most SWT form failures aren’t word count — they’re two sentences masquerading as one (‘…districts. However, costs…’). Proofread hunting specifically for a second capital letter after a full stop. One capital, one stop. Binary.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT reading: once, with intent

    10 minutes = ~3 min reading, ~5 min writing, ~2 min checking. Read once for structure (thesis? evidence? conclusion?), not twice for detail. The marks live in the writing and checking phases — protect them.

    Summarize Written Text

    Word-count discipline

    The on-screen counter is your friend — check it before submitting. Aim 55–65 words: safely above the 5-word floor, far from the 75-word ceiling, with room for a checking pass. Form failures are the most avoidable zeros in the test.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT vs SST: don’t mix them up

    SWT (writing task): ONE sentence, 5–75 words, from a written passage. SST (listening task): 50–70 words, multiple sentences, from audio. Different inputs, different shapes — training one doesn’t train the other. Practise both shapes deliberately.

    Summarize Written Text

    Qualification: the SWT secret weapon

    Strong SWT sentences carry the passage’s caveats: ‘although’, ‘while’, ‘despite’. Passages almost always argue two-sided — benefit AND cost, trend AND exception. A summary with only one side loses content. Hunt the ‘but’.

    Summarize Written Text

    Grammar under pressure

    SWT grammar fails cluster on: articles, subject-verb agreement, clause linkage. In your 2-minute check, read the sentence backwards clause-by-clause — it breaks your ‘I know what I meant’ blindness and exposes the slips.

    Summarize Written Text 30 September 2026

    The 60-word editorial drill

    Weekly: take a news editorial → compress to one 60-word sentence → verify form. Ten reps and the SWT shape becomes muscle memory. This single drill has rescued more SWT scores than any amount of theory.

    Write Essay

    One essay, full weight

    1 essay × 20 minutes, 200–300 words — the longest sustained writing output in the test, now carrying more weight after the August 2025 scoring changes. Templates, timing discipline and idea banks matter more than ever.

    Write Essay

    The 4-paragraph engine

    Intro (paraphrase + position) → Body 1 (one idea, developed) → Body 2 (one idea, developed) → Conclusion (restate + recap). This shape directly serves the Development/Structure/Coherence trait — the scorer can follow your argument without effort.

    Write Essay

    PEEL your paragraphs

    Point → Explain → Example → Link. ‘Online learning helps rural students (P) because it removes distance barriers (E) — e.g. streamed lectures reached villages during school closures (Ex), making education more equitable (L).’ Developed beats asserted, every time.

    Write Essay

    The 200-word floor

    199 words = Form zero. The cruellest rule in the test. Always write to ~240: a miscount still clears the floor, and you stay far under the 300 ceiling. Count during your checking minutes — the counter is displayed, no excuses.

    Write Essay

    Vocabulary range without showing off

    Range rewards precise academic collocations used correctly — ‘significant drawback’, ‘compelling evidence’, ‘plays a crucial role’. Misused rare words score worse than well-used common ones. Build a bank of 60 safe collocations and deploy them.

    Write Essay

    Templates are scaffolding, not cheating

    Functional frames (‘This essay will argue that…’, ‘A key advantage is…’, ‘In conclusion…’) are legitimate. What’s punished: reciting a memorised essay off-topic. Templates organise YOUR ideas about THIS prompt — they don’t replace them.

    Write Essay

    Answer the actual question

    Prompts have instruction words: discuss both views, give your opinion, suggest solutions. A brilliant one-sided essay on a discuss-both-views prompt fails Content. Underline the instruction words in planning — 30 seconds that saves the essay.

    Write Essay

    The 20-minute split

    3–4 min plan (position + 2 points + examples) → 13–14 min write → 2–3 min check (count, paragraphs, articles, spelling). Unplanned essays ramble; unchecked essays bleed grammar. The split is non-negotiable.

    Write Essay

    Spelling: pick a side

    British or American — both accepted, but mixing (‘organise’ + ‘organize’) costs Spelling-trait marks. Choose in practice, stay loyal on test day. Your personal top-20 misspellings list is worth more than any generic one.

    Write Essay

    Coherence glue

    Linkers make logic visible: however, moreover, consequently, for instance, in contrast. One per 2–3 sentences is the natural density — more feels mechanical, fewer feels disjointed. Variety matters: don’t ‘however’ five times.

    Reading

    FIB: the Reading jackpot

    5–6 R&W texts + 5–6 R texts per test, 1 point per blank, zero penalty. FIB blanks are the single biggest Reading contributor — and R&W also feeds Writing. An hour spent on collocations and word-forms here outscores an hour anywhere else in Reading.

    Reading

    Grammar slot first

    Half of FIB gaps resolve by grammar alone: after ‘will’ → base verb; after ‘the’ → noun; ‘___ly’ → adverb. Check the slot before the meaning — it eliminates 2–3 options instantly. Word-family sets (consider/consideration/considerable) are the classic design.

    Reading

    Collocation slot second

    The other half resolve by fixed phrases: ‘draw a conclusion’, ‘take into account’, ‘play a vital role’, ‘come into effect’. 200 high-frequency academic collocations memorised = most FIB items feel like recognition, not reasoning.

    Reading

    Re-order: the pronoun chain

    ‘They argued…’, ‘this discovery…’, ‘however…’ — pronouns and connectors point backwards. Find what each refers to and the paragraph order assembles itself. It’s the most mechanical task in Reading: chain the references, collect the pair points.

    Reading

    The topic-sentence test

    The true opener stands alone: general statement, no ‘this/they/however’ pointing at unseen text. When two paragraphs both look first-ish, the one with backward references is the continuation. This single check resolves most opener disputes.

    Reading

    MCQ multiple: the discipline

    +1 / −1, floor 0. Rule: only tick what the text directly supports. The test plants ‘mentioned but irrelevant’ options — recognising a phrase isn’t enough; it must answer the question asked. When torn on a third tick, leave it: two safe > three risky.

    Reading

    Question-first scanning

    For MCQs: read the question stem first, then hunt the passage for that specific thing (a cause? an attitude? a number?). Deep-reading everything burns the 30-minute budget; targeted scanning finishes with time to spare. The text — never general knowledge — is the authority.

    Reading

    Reading time budget

    ~30 minutes for ~15–17 items: ~2 min per FIB text (high value), brisk single MCQs, steady re-orders. Start the clock mentally at item one — the section ends hard, and unfinished items are zeros you chose.

    Reading

    Academic register: meet it where it lives

    PTE Reading isn’t conversational English — it’s abstract nouns, nominalisations, formal collocations. Daily diet: editorials, popular science, textbook chapters. Fifteen minutes a day for a month rewires your reading brain to the test’s frequency.

    Reading 30 September 2026

    The emergency protocol

    3 minutes left, items untouched? Guess every FIB blank, quick-pick single MCQs — no negative marking, blanks are pure loss. Only multiple-answer MCQs need caution. Never, ever leave scorable items empty.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    SST: lecture to prose

    60–90s audio → 50–70 words, 10 min. Listen with full attention (no writing sentences mid-audio), capture 8–12 keywords, then write 3–4 sentences: main message first, then supports. Feeds Writing + Listening across four traits — elite value per minute.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    Front-load the thesis

    Open your SST with the lecturer’s main message. If memory or word count fails later, the core content point is banked. Inverted pyramid: most important first — content scoring rewards exactly this prioritisation.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    SST form check

    50–70 words — 44 fails form. Count before submitting; the counter is on screen. One more sentence with a supporting detail fixes a short draft; filler never does. Two minutes of checking protects a ten-minute investment.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    MCQ preview: the free seconds

    The 5–7 seconds before audio are free prep: read the question, skim options, decide what to listen for (cause? number? attitude?). Targeted listening catches what blind listening misses — use every pre-audio pause.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    Attitude questions: listen for evaluatives

    Speakers signal stance with word choice, not announcements: ‘unfortunately’, ‘remarkable’, ‘however’, hedges and intensifiers. Note adjectives and turn-markers as you listen — attitude items are decided by tone-words.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    The distractor design

    Listening MCQs mention every option so recognition alone fails. The winner is the option that answers the question asked — cause vs effect, problem vs solution. Anchor on the stem while listening; don’t just collect mentions.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    Notes: match to the question

    Detail/number questions → jot figures. Gist/attitude questions → listen fully, minimal notes. Scribbling through a tone question divides the exact attention tone requires. Preview the question, then choose your mode.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    Typing: the hidden skill

    SST and WFD need steady, accurate typing — 30–40 wpm with good accuracy beats hunt-and-peck panic. Practise by typing (not handwriting) your practice summaries. Key-hunting burns the 10-minute SST window.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    The missed-seconds recovery

    Zoned out for 5 seconds? Stay in the item. MCQs test gist and arc, not single moments — re-engage and the question usually still answers itself. Switching off guarantees the loss; recovering usually saves it.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    Train at test speed

    Podcasts at natural speed, varied accents — not 1.5x. Overspeed training builds skimming habits that miss the qualifiers PTE options hinge on. The test plays once, at natural pace: rehearse that reality.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    HCS: spot the difference

    Four summaries, 1–2 words apart (‘increased’ vs ‘decreased’, ‘argues’ vs ‘questions’). Pre-read to extract the decision points, then listen discriminatively for exactly those. Careful listening for the exact claim — that’s the whole task.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Stance verbs decide

    ‘Argues/supports’ vs ‘questions/criticises’ must match the speaker’s real position. Doubtful tone → ‘questions’. These single-word stance differences are the most common decider in Highlight Correct Summary — listen for attitude, not just facts.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Missing Word: ride the momentum

    The sentence builds toward its ending: logic + intonation + collocation nearly announce the missing word (‘trend… likely to ___ → continue’). Listen to the whole arc, not just the beep. 1 point, no penalty — always answer.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Listening FIB: spell or fail

    You hear the word — now spell it exactly. ‘Accomodation’ scores zero. The gaps are fair academic words made hard by speed. Keep a personal misspelling hit-list and drill it; spelling is the entire game here. Since 2025: Listening score only.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Follow-and-type

    Eyes track the transcript slightly ahead of the audio; type each missing word the instant you hear it. Waiting for the audio to end forces reliance on fading memory of exact forms. The professional technique is rhythmic, not retrospective.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Dictation: the 10-minute daily fix

    Short academic clips → type every content word → check spelling. This trains hear → recognise → spell under pressure — the exact Listening FIB chain. Films with subtitles don’t stress it; targeted dictation does.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Light notes for light items

    HCS and Missing Word need only difference-anchors (the 2–3 differing claims), not full notes. Heavy note-taking pulls attention from the single decision that matters. Save the heavy notes for SST and Retell Lecture.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Qualifier ears

    ‘Always’ vs ‘sometimes’, ‘all’ vs ‘most’, ‘increased’ vs ‘decreased’ — polarity and qualifier words decide HCS and Missing Word items. Train your ear to perk up at them; they’re quiet but they’re the test.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    The listening section map

    ~30–43 min: SST, MCQs, FIB, HCS, Missing Word, HIW, WFD. It’s the final section — fatigue management is real. Spend your freshest focus on SST, FIB and WFD (highest value); cruise the lighter MCQs.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing 30 September 2026

    One hearing only

    No replays anywhere in Listening. That’s why preview seconds, note discipline and recovery skills matter more than raw comprehension. The test rewards procedure under pressure as much as language itself — rehearse the procedures.

    HIW & Dictation

    WFD: the bankable task

    Hear once → type exactly. 3–4 sentences, 8–15 words each, 1 point per correct word in order, no negative marking, feeds Listening + Writing. Format never changes; skill is pure trainable. Two weeks of daily dictation converts WFD into your most reliable points.

    HIW & Dictation

    Chunk, don’t type-and-listen

    Listen fully → hold as 2–3 phrase-chunks → type. Typing while listening garbles both channels. Your short-term memory comfortably holds a 12-word sentence as chunks — trust it. The pause after each sentence is for checking, not recovering.

    HIW & Dictation

    Small words, full points

    ‘a’, ‘the’, plural ‘-s’ — every token is a point. Dropping small words is the classic WFD leak: 9 right content words out of 12 still loses 3 points on articles and endings. In your check-pass, hunt specifically for the little words.

    HIW & Dictation

    Homophones: let grammar decide

    ‘Affect’ vs ‘effect’, ‘their’ vs ‘there’, ‘its’ vs ‘it’s’ — the sentence slot disambiguates: verb position → affect, noun position → effect. Drill confusable pairs by grammar slot, not by sound. Sound is what confused you.

    HIW & Dictation

    HIW: the click discipline

    +1 per true mismatch, −1 per wrong click, floor 0. Golden rule: click only sure mismatches. Speculative clicking erases genuine finds. 2–3 items per test, feeding Listening + Reading — precision over enthusiasm.

    HIW & Dictation

    HIW mismatches are lexical

    Click on different words (‘economic’ vs ‘economical’), not accented pronunciations of the right word. An unfamiliar accent saying the correct word is NOT a click — that’s a −1 trap. Ear for wording, tolerance for accent.

    HIW & Dictation

    Cursor tracking

    Follow the HIW text with your cursor just ahead of the audio so each word arrives under attention exactly as it sounds. Mismatches then pop out. A static cursor forces visual search; random clicking triggers the penalty. Rhythm wins.

    HIW & Dictation

    The WFD check-pass

    After typing: re-read for spelling, articles, plurals, word order, capitalisation (‘Thursday’ not ‘thursday’). Most lost WFD points are checkable slips, not memory failures. The pause between sentences exists for this — use it.

    HIW & Dictation

    Never submit blanks

    WFD has no negative marking: a guessed word can only add. Confident on 10 of 12 words? Guess the other two. Gaps score zero with certainty; guesses score sometimes. The math is unambiguous — complete every sentence.

    HIW & Dictation

    Verbatim or nothing

    WFD doesn’t paraphrase-grade: ‘about’ ≠ ‘on’, ‘was postponed’ ≠ ‘has been postponed’. Exact words, exact order. Train with strict self-marking — generous marking in practice builds habits the scorer won’t reward.

    Vocabulary

    The academic word list

    PTE’s register runs on the Academic Word List family: analyse, significant, phenomenon, implications, controversy. ~570 word families cover a huge share of Reading and Listening. Learn them as families (analyse/analysis/analytical), not isolated words.

    Vocabulary

    Collocations beat definitions

    Knowing ‘draw’ + ‘conclusion’ separately doesn’t help FIB — knowing ‘draw a conclusion’ does. Study vocabulary in chunks: verb+noun, adjective+noun pairs. 200 collocations transform FIB, WFD-adjacent spelling, and essay vocabulary range together.

    Vocabulary

    Word families for FIB

    FIB banks love word-family sets: consider (v) / consideration (n) / considerable (adj) / considerably (adv). When all four appear as options, the grammar slot picks the winner. Learn suffixes: -tion (noun), -ive/-al (adj), -ly (adv), -ise (verb).

    Vocabulary

    The 20-minute vocabulary loop

    Daily: 10 new collocations (write 2 example sentences each) + 10-minute review of yesterday’s (cover, recall, check). Spaced, productive, small. Vocabulary built this way survives test-day pressure; list-memorised words evaporate.

    Vocabulary

    False friends and confusables

    ‘Sensible’ ≠ sensitive; ‘actual’ = real (not ‘current’); ‘sympathetic’ = compassionate. Keep a confusables notebook — these pairs are over-represented in FIB distractors and ASQ traps precisely because learners mix them.

    Vocabulary

    Prefixes decode unknowns

    Meet an unknown word? Dissect it: un-/in-/dis- (not), re- (again), pre- (before), -ology (study of). ‘Deindustrialisation’ surrenders to prefix-stripping. In Reading you rarely need the full word — the direction (positive/negative, increase/decrease) is usually enough.

    Vocabulary

    Nominalisation: the academic fingerprint

    Academic English turns verbs into nouns: ‘implement’ → ‘implementation’, ‘fail’ → ‘failure’. Recognising the verb inside the noun unlocks dense sentences fast. FIB gaps are frequently the nominalised form — train the transformation both ways.

    Vocabulary

    Synonym ladders for paraphrase

    SWT, SST and Retell reward paraphrase: build ladders — increase → rise → grow → climb; important → crucial → vital → pivotal. Four rungs per common concept, practised in sentences. Paraphrase fluency is a trainable muscle.

    Vocabulary

    Spelling demons list

    Everyone has 20–30 personal demons: accommodation, necessary, occurrence, separate, definitely… Yours differ from everyone else’s. Harvest them from your own WFD and FIB errors — a personal list beats any generic ‘top 100 misspellings’.

    Vocabulary 30 September 2026

    Context over translation

    Learn new words in English sentences, not via translation pairs. Translation gives one meaning; context gives usage, collocation and register — the three things PTE actually tests. Monolingual learner dictionaries beat bilingual lists for test prep.

    Test Day & Strategy

    The week before

    Taper, don’t cram. Light review of templates and error lists; one full mock mid-week; sleep 8 hours nightly. Last-minute heavy study raises anxiety without adding skill. Your score on test day reflects the last month, not the last night.

    Test Day & Strategy

    Test-day kit

    Passport/ID (the one you registered with — no alternatives), confirmation email, water and snack for before/after. Arrive 30 minutes early. Phones and watches stay in the locker — the centre provides everything else, including the noise.

    Test Day & Strategy

    The tutorial is free time

    The pre-test tutorial and mic check are unscored — use them to settle: breathe, set mic position, test volume, rehearse your RA opening line mentally. Rushing the tutorial starts the test rattled; using it starts you calibrated.

    Test Day & Strategy

    Speaking first: use it

    Part 1 opens with speaking while you’re fresh — by design. Warm up your voice in the waiting area (quietly!): read a paragraph aloud. A warmed voice articulates cleaner from item one; a cold voice stumbles the first two items.

    Test Day & Strategy

    The 2-hour energy plan

    No scheduled breaks: pace your intensity. Full focus on high-value items (RA, RS, FIB, SST, WFD); efficient cruising on lighter MCQs. A 10-second eyes-closed reset between sections is legal and restorative — use it.

    Test Day & Strategy

    When disaster strikes mid-test

    Botched an item? It’s one item. Integrated scoring means recovery paths exist everywhere; panic about item 5 destroys items 6–10. Reset ritual: breath, roll shoulders, next item gets 100%. Compartmentalise ruthlessly.

    Test Day & Strategy

    The 79-each strategy

    Chasing 79 in each skill? Your plan differs: zero weak tasks matters more than brilliant ones — one collapsed section caps the skill. Drill your worst task type first every session; the ceiling is set by the floor, not the peak.

    Test Day & Strategy

    The 50-each strategy

    Need 50s? Master the bankable core: RA accuracy, RS chunking, FIB collocations, WFD dictation, SWT form. These five task families alone can carry every skill past 50. Depth on high-yield tasks beats breadth on everything.

    Test Day & Strategy

    Mock tests: how to use them

    One full timed mock per week, then 2 hours of error analysis per mock. Log every error by task type; the pattern (not the score) is the product. Three analysed mocks beat ten unreviewed ones — review is where the points are.

    Test Day & Strategy 30 September 2026

    Results and what’s next

    Results typically land within 48 hours (often same-day). Download the score report, map weak skills to task types, and retake with a targeted plan — not ‘more practice’, but practice aimed at the specific leaks the report implies.

    Format & Scoring

    Score report anatomy

    Your report shows overall (10–90) plus four communicative scores. Institutions set per-skill minimums too — a 70 overall with Listening 52 can still miss a ‘65 each’ requirement. Always check whether your target wants overall only or each-skill bands.

    Format & Scoring

    Why PTE suits fast results

    Frequent test dates, fast results (often <48h), fully computerised — PTE’s logistics beat most rivals for tight deadlines. If your visa clock is ticking, PTE’s turnaround is a strategic advantage, not just a convenience.

    Format & Scoring

    PTE Academic vs PTE Core

    Academic = study/visa for Australia, UK, NZ, Canada. PTE Core = Canadian immigration (IRCC). Same engine, different purpose — book the wrong one and your scores go nowhere. Confirm which your institution/authority accepts before paying.

    Format & Scoring

    The reschedule rules

    Life happens: check Pearson’s reschedule/cancellation windows when booking — fees depend on notice period. Knowing the policy in advance turns a crisis into an admin task. Screenshot your booking confirmation.

    Read Aloud

    RA: linking and chunking

    Natural speech links words (‘pick_it_up’) and groups them in phrases. Read-aloud fluency is judged on this connectedness — word-by-word delivery, however accurate, sounds hesitant. Practise reading with a pencil marking phrase boundaries.

    Read Aloud

    RA: the difficult-word protocol

    During prep, sound out hard words silently once, then move on. Decide a ‘good enough’ pronunciation and commit — mid-recording experimentation breaks rhythm. One confident attempt beats three hesitant ones.

    Read Aloud

    RA: volume consistency

    Keep one steady volume start to finish. Trailing off at sentence ends (a common habit) drops final words below the engine’s threshold — they score as omissions. Finish every sentence at full voice, then pause silently.

    Read Aloud

    RA: the newsreader mindset

    Imagine reading the evening news: clear, paced, mildly interested, zero drama. That register — not conversation, not speech-making — is what the fluency and pronunciation traits are calibrated on. Adopt the persona; the scores follow.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    RS: the 3-second launch

    After the beep you get ~3 seconds before the mic opens — use them to fix your first chunk in mind, not to panic. Starting promptly with a confident first phrase sets the fluency tone for the whole repeat. Hesitant starts cascade.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    RS: articles are content

    ‘A’ vs ‘the’, singular vs plural — each is a content point. When rebuilding from notes, default to what you actually heard, not what ‘sounds right’. Your grammar instinct will ‘correct’ the sentence into wrongness. Trust the ear.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    DI: the comparison engine

    Comparisons are content gold: ‘twice as high as’, ‘marginally above’, ‘in stark contrast to’. Memorise 15 comparison frames and DI/RL responses gain instant structure. Numbers without comparison are just reading aloud.

    Repeat Sentence & Describe Image

    DI: pie charts in 40 seconds

    Pies: largest slice → smallest → notable shares → what it means. Percentages are the content — ‘X dominates at 42%, more than double Y’s 18%’. Don’t list all six slices; rank and compare.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    RL: the lecture arc

    Most lectures follow problem → evidence → implication (or past → present → future). Hearing the arc lets you predict structure and note efficiently. When lost, ask: ‘where in the arc am I?’ — it re-orients instantly.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    RL: numbers are anchors

    A lecture’s figures stick in memory better than abstractions — and they’re concrete content points. Note every number with its label (‘40% → dropout rate’). In the retell, numbers make vague recall sound precise.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ: the category sweep

    ASQ questions cluster in categories: geography, science, occupations, instruments, time. Weak in one category? Ten targeted flashcards fix it in a day. Categorise your misses and the drill writes itself.

    Retell Lecture & Answer Short Q

    ASQ: pronunciation doesn’t matter (here)

    The only PTE speaking task where pronunciation is unscored — mumbling the right word still banks the point. Don’t polish delivery on ASQ; spend that energy on instant retrieval. Right word, fast.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT: the length ladder

    Can’t hit 60 words? Build up: write 30 → add a clause → add a qualification. Each rung is one subordinator (‘although’, ‘which’, ‘because’). The ladder turns ‘too short’ into ‘just right’ mechanically.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT: verb choice carries meaning

    Strong SWT verbs do heavy lifting: ‘underscores’, ‘attributes’, ‘contends’, ‘reveals’. One precise verb replaces a clause. Build a 30-verb academic arsenal — it pays in SWT, SST and essays simultaneously.

    Summarize Written Text

    SWT: the ‘so what’ test

    After drafting, ask: ‘does this capture why the passage matters?’ Summaries that list facts but miss the implication lose content. The passage’s ‘so what’ — usually in the final paragraph — belongs in your sentence.

    Write Essay

    Essay: the opinion tightrope

    ‘Discuss both views and give your opinion’: fair to both, clear on yours. Burying your stance loses task-response marks; ignoring the other side loses balance marks. Formula: view A fairly → view B fairly → ‘I lean to B because…’.

    Write Essay

    Essay: examples that work

    Examples needn’t be real statistics — plausible specifics (‘in my city, evening classes filled within days’) develop points fine. What fails: vague hand-waving (‘many people think so’). Concrete > grandiose, always.

    Write Essay

    Essay: the concession move

    Top-band essays concede then rebut: ‘Admittedly, X has costs; however, the benefits outweigh them because…’. This single move demonstrates critical thinking — the Development trait’s favourite flavour. Use it once per essay.

    Reading

    Reading FIB: the double-duty blank

    Some blanks test grammar AND collocation together — only one option fits both. When two options survive the grammar check, the collocation decides (‘draw’ + ‘conclusion’, not ‘pull’). Layer the filters: slot first, phrase second.

    Reading

    Re-order: time markers

    Dates and sequence words (1998, ‘initially’, ‘subsequently’, ‘finally’) hand you chronological order directly. Scan for them before deep reading — they solve or half-solve most items mechanically.

    Reading

    Re-order: the conclusion test

    The true ending often summarises or projects (‘In summary…’, ‘Future research…’) and refers back to everything. If a paragraph feels like a wrap-up but contains forward pointers, it’s the end — anchor it and build backwards.

    Reading

    MCQ single: the 50/50 split

    Down to two options? Re-read the question stem, not the options — the stem’s exact wording (‘suggests’ vs ‘states’) usually kills one. Test-takers who re-read options loop; those who re-read the question decide.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    SST: the sentence budget

    50–70 words ≈ 3–4 sentences. Budget them: 1 = main message, 2–3 = key supports, 4 (optional) = implication/example. A 5th sentence risks the word cap; a 2-sentence summary risks thin content. Four is the sweet spot.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    SST: tense consistency

    Pick a tense frame and hold it: lectures describe past research or present facts. Tense-hopping (‘found… finds… will find’) bleeds Grammar marks for zero content gain. Decide in sentence one; maintain.

    Listening: SST & MCQs

    MCQ listening: the first-listen rule

    There are no replays — so commit on first hearing. Second-guessing from fading memory performs worse than trusting the initial impression. Mark your answer and move on; ruminating steals the next item’s preview seconds.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    HCS: the ‘too specific’ trap

    Wrong options often add unmentioned specifics — precise numbers or claims the speaker never made. If an option’s detail doesn’t ring a bell from the audio, distrust it. The right summary stays at the speaker’s level of generality.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    Missing Word: the grammar finish

    Endings constrain answers: after ‘to’ comes a base verb; after ‘the’ a noun. When logic leaves two candidates, grammar picks. The beep hides one word — the sentence’s grammar never lies about its shape.

    Listening: FIB, Summary & Missing

    FIB listening: the content-word focus

    Gaps are content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) — never ‘the’ or ‘and’. So listen for the stressed words; English stress falls on content. Your ear already knows where to listen — trust the stress pattern.

    HIW & Dictation

    WFD: the clause-boundary chunk

    Long sentence? Split at the clause boundary (‘…policy, which was introduced in 2019, has…’ → two chunks). Each chunk is independently memorable. Chunking at grammar joints beats chunking at arbitrary word counts.

    HIW & Dictation

    WFD: the capitalisation check

    Days, months, names, sentence starts — capitals are spelling points. ‘thursday’ loses what ‘Thursday’ earns. Add a capitalisation sweep to every check-pass; it’s the cheapest point in the task.

    HIW & Dictation

    HIW: the pace trap

    Speakers in HIW read at natural speed — if your cursor lags, mismatches slip past unclicked. Practise tracking audiobook text at 1x until following feels effortless. The skill is fluency of tracking, not speed of clicking.

    Vocabulary

    Vocabulary: the 5-a-day contract

    Five collocations a day, used in your own sentences, reviewed weekly. In 60 days: 300 collocations — enough to transform FIB, essays and SST vocabulary traits together. Small, daily, non-negotiable beats weekend binges.

    Vocabulary

    Vocabulary: read what you’ll be tested on

    Your reading diet is your vocabulary program: science news, editorials, economics explainers. Words met in context stick; words met in lists don’t. Fifteen minutes of real reading daily outperforms an hour of flashcards weekly.

    Test Day & Strategy

    Test day: the ID rule

    Bring the exact ID you registered with — usually a passport. Name mismatches or expired documents mean no test, no refund. Check it the night before; this is the most boring and most important capsule here.

    Test Day & Strategy

    After the test: score use

    Send scores via your Pearson account to institutions — most accept electronic delivery directly. Check each institution’s code and requirements before sending; resends are possible but slow. Keep your login safe.

    Write from Dictation is the highest-weight item in PTE — it feeds both Listening and Writing. Type what you hear at speed, then spend the last seconds fixing articles (a/an/the) and plural -s. These two tiny fixes convert near-misses into full marks because each correct word scores.
